Quick answer: Vintage buyers do not search the way ordinary buyers search. They search by era, by style, by material and by the story of the item — "1970s brass lamp", "Art Deco brooch", "Mid-Century teak side table" — and the listings they click are the ones that answer those searches with evidence: an era word the seller can support, a title that names the item first and the era second, tags that cover the item, the era, the material and the use, photos that show the condition honestly, and a description that names the wear, the marks and the measurements a buyer needs before they can buy a one-of-a-kind item unseen. Why vintage SEO is different from ordinary Etsy SEO

A new handmade item can be described in general words, because the buyer knows what a new item is: it will be as described, in the size stated, with no history and no wear. A vintage item is the opposite. It is one of a kind, it has a history, and its condition is part of what the buyer is buying — sometimes the most interesting part. That changes what the buyer searches for, and it changes what the listing must say. A buyer looking for a new ceramic mug searches for the mug. A buyer looking for a vintage ceramic mug searches for the era, the maker, the pattern, the colourway and the condition, because those are the details that separate the mug they want from the hundreds of similar mugs that are not quite right. The search phrases get longer and more specific as the buyer gets more serious: first "vintage mug", then "1970s stoneware mug", then "1970s stoneware mug brown glaze" — and the listing that is found at the last, most specific stage is the listing that was written with that detail in it. The second difference is trust. A vintage buyer is buying something they cannot examine in person, from a seller they have never met, and the item cannot be replaced if it arrives in a worse condition than described. The buyer therefore reads the listing the way they would examine the item in a shop: they look for the marks, the wear, the repairs, the measurements and the maker's mark, and they judge the seller by how honestly those details are shown. A listing that hides the wear will be passed over by the careful buyer and returned by the careless one. A listing that names the wear plainly, photographs it, and explains what it means for using the item will be trusted — and trusted listings are the ones vintage buyers return to, because a vintage buyer who finds an honest seller becomes a repeat buyer in a way that rarely happens with new goods. The SEO in this guide is built on that trust: every keyword choice, every title and every tag starts from evidence about the item, and the evidence is shown, not just claimed.

What counts as vintage — and why getting it right matters

Etsy defines vintage items as items that are at least 20 years old. That definition is the line between a vintage listing and a listing that should be described differently, and it matters for search as well as for honesty: buyers who filter or search for vintage items expect genuine older items, and a shop that calls new items vintage will lose those buyers quickly and permanently. Before you use the word vintage for an item, check the age from the item itself. Look for a maker's mark, a manufacturer's label, a date stamp, a patent number, a style detail that belongs to a particular period, or paperwork that came with the item. Compare the mark and the style with reference books, museum collections and reputable identification guides, and keep a note of what you found, because that note becomes the description. If the evidence points to a decade, you can use that decade. If it points to a period — Art Deco, Mid-Century, post-war — you can use that period. If the evidence is unclear, say so honestly: describe the item, describe the clues, and let the buyer decide, rather than guessing a decade that the item cannot support. The words vintage, antique and retro are not interchangeable, and using them carelessly will bring the wrong buyers. Antique usually means much older than vintage, and should only be used when the item truly is of that age. Retro usually means a newer item made in an older style, and it should not be used for a genuine vintage item if it suggests the item is a reproduction. Vintage-style or vintage-inspired should be used for new items made to look old, and those items should not be listed as vintage at all. The safest rule is simple: use the word that the evidence supports, and explain the evidence in the description. A buyer who understands why you call an item 1960s will trust the rest of the listing too, and a buyer who sees the word vintage used for everything will trust nothing in the shop.

Era words: the vocabulary vintage buyers actually type

Era words are the heart of vintage SEO, because they are the words that turn a general search into a vintage search. Buyers use era words in two ways: as decades — 1920s, 1950s, 1970s, 1980s — and as named periods and styles — Art Deco, Art Nouveau, Mid-Century Modern, Bauhaus, Victorian, Edwardian, Retro, Space Age, Hollywood Regency. The decade words are the most searched and the most useful, because a buyer who knows the decade they want will type it first. The named styles are just as valuable for the items they fit, because a buyer who knows they want Art Deco will type Art Deco rather than a decade, and a listing that uses the style name will be found while a listing that only uses a decade will not. Use both where both are true: a 1930s brooch in the Art Deco style should carry both the decade and the style, in the title and in the tags, because different buyers will search by each. The era words must be earned, as the last section explained. A lamp from the 1970s can be described as 1970s and as Space Age if its shape and materials support it. A lamp made last year in a 1970s style cannot be described as 1970s, however much it looks the part. Beyond the era itself, vintage buyers search by the language of the period: the materials of the time — Bakelite, teak, brass, chrome, lucite, cork, rattan — the colours of the time — avocado, harvest gold, burnt orange, olive — and the makers and brands of the time, where the item carries a maker's name. Those words belong in the tags and in the description, because they are the words that separate one vintage item from another. Research them the way our keyword research guide describes: type the item words into Etsy's search bar, then type the item words with a decade, then with a style, and read the autocomplete suggestions at each stage. The suggestions are the phrases real buyers have typed, and the phrases that appear at every stage are the phrases to build the listing around. Keep a keyword list per era as well as per item, because a shop that sells 1950s and 1970s items will use different era words for each, and the list stops the wrong era word creeping into the wrong listing.

Titles: item first, era second, evidence throughout

The title is where the era word does its most visible work, and the rule is the same as for any Etsy title — item first — with the era word immediately after it. A buyer searching for a lamp needs to see that the listing is a lamp before they care what decade it is from, and a buyer searching for a 1970s lamp needs to see both words together to know this listing is the one. "1970s Brass Table Lamp — Space Age Desk Lamp, Vintage Lighting" works because the first words say what it is and when it is from, and the words after it add the style and the use. "Vintage Retro Lamp Old Antique Brass" does not work, because it says the item is old in four different ways and never quite says what it is, and because it uses words — retro, antique — that the evidence may not support. Keep the title readable as a description a person would understand, and use the era word once, in its natural place, rather than repeating it. The rest of the title should carry the details that help a buyer decide: the material, the colour, the maker if the item has one, and the use or the room it suits. "Mid-Century Teak Side Table — 1960s Danish Style Coffee Table, Vintage Wooden Table" tells the buyer the style, the material, the decade and the use in one line, and it does so without repeating any word. Avoid the two title habits that damage vintage listings most. The first is stuffing the title with every era word that might apply — 1950s, 1960s, 1970s, Art Deco, Mid-Century — in the hope of being found for all of them. A listing found for a search it does not match will be passed over, and a title that claims five eras tells the buyer the seller does not know which era the item is from. The second is hiding the era altogether, in the hope that the item words alone will be enough. They will not, because the item words alone put the listing in competition with every new item of the same kind, and the vintage buyer will scroll past them looking for the era word. One era, supported by evidence, placed after the item, is the pattern to follow. Our guide to Etsy titles, tags and descriptions covers the full title method, and the vintage version is that method with the era word given its proper place — second, prominent, and earned.

Tags: thirteen slots for item, era, material, style and use

The tags are where the full vintage vocabulary lives, because the title can only carry the item and the era while the tags can carry everything else a buyer might type. Use all thirteen tags, and build them in groups from the item in front of you. The first group is the item words: the main item phrase and its closest alternatives — "brass table lamp", "vintage desk lamp", "table lamp brass". The second group is the era words: the decade and the named style — "1970s lamp", "space age lamp", "mid century lighting" — used only where the evidence supports them. The third group is the material and colour words: "brass", "brass and glass", "amber glass", "burnt orange" — the details a buyer uses to narrow a search when the item words alone return too many results. The fourth group is the style and use words: "statement lamp", "bedside lamp", "reading lamp", "living room lighting" — the words that describe how and where the item will be used. The fifth group, where it fits, is the maker and origin words: the maker's name, the country of origin, or the workshop, where the item carries them. Do not repeat the same phrase in several tags, and do not use a tag for a phrase the item does not deserve — a tag is a promise that the listing matches it. In particular, do not use a decade tag for a decade the item is not from, and do not use a maker tag for a maker the item is not by, because the buyer who searches for that maker will arrive expecting that maker and leave disappointed. Check the tag set against the title, so the two work together: the title carries the main item phrase and the main era phrase, and the tags carry the rest of the vocabulary, including the phrases that would crowd the title. Our guide to filling your 13 tags shows the full method, and the vintage set is that method applied to an item with a history — the era, material and style groups matter more here than for a new item, because they are the groups the vintage buyer searches by. Keep the tag set for each listing with your notes on the item, so that when a similar item arrives you can start from a proven set rather than from scratch — but always check the set against the new item, because two similar-looking vintage items can be from different decades, and the tags must follow the item, not the last listing.

Condition: describe the wear, photograph the wear, explain the wear

Condition is where vintage listings are won and lost, because it is the one thing a buyer cannot check for themselves and the one thing that most determines whether they will be happy when the item arrives. A condition description should do three things: name the wear, show the wear, and explain what the wear means for using the item. Name it in plain words, and name where it is: "light scratches to the brass base, most visible on the back", "a small chip to the rim, on the right side when facing the front", "fading to the fabric on the left arm, consistent with age and use". Avoid condition words on their own. "Good condition" tells the buyer nothing, because one seller's good is another seller's fair, and a buyer who has been disappointed before will read it as a warning. "Good condition for its age, with light wear to the base as photographed" tells the buyer what to expect and where to look for the evidence. Show the wear in the photos, in close-up, with the same honesty as the words — a photo of the chip, a photo of the scratch, a photo of the repair — placed after the photos that show the item at its best, so the buyer sees the item first and the wear second, but sees both before buying. Explain what the wear means: does it affect how the item works, does it affect how it looks from a normal distance, has it been repaired, and if so, how and by whom? A repair that is named and photographed is a fact about the item's history; a repair that is discovered on arrival is a betrayal. The same honesty applies to what is missing: a lamp without its original shade, a set with one piece replaced, a watch without its original strap should say so plainly, because the buyer who wanted the complete item will pass, and the buyer who is happy with the item as it is will buy with confidence. If an item needs work before it can be used — rewiring, cleaning, a new part — say so, and say what work is needed, so the buyer can judge the true cost. A condition description written this way will lose some buyers, and that is the point: it loses the buyers who would have been disappointed, and it keeps the buyers who wanted a real vintage item, with real history, described honestly. Those are the buyers who leave the reviews a vintage shop lives on.

Descriptions: provenance, measurements and the story that is true

The description is where the vintage listing becomes a story — but it must be a true story, told from evidence, not a romance invented to sell the item. Start with the item itself: what it is, when it is from, who made it if that is known, and what makes it interesting. Then give the facts a buyer needs before they can buy a one-of-a-kind item unseen. Measurements come first among them, and they must be complete: height, width and depth for furniture and lighting, diameter for round items, length and width for textiles, and the measurements that matter for the item's use — the shade height for a lamp, the internal size for a bag, the wrist size for a watch. Give measurements in the units your buyers use, and check them twice, because a buyer cannot try the item on or hold it up to the space it will fill, and a wrong measurement is the most common cause of a vintage return. State the materials plainly, including the materials that are not obvious from the photos — the lining of a bag, the backing of a mirror, the filling of a cushion — and state anything the buyer needs to know about care: whether the item can be cleaned, how it should be stored, and whether it needs specialist care. Then the provenance: where the item came from, as far as you know it, and what evidence supports the date and the maker. "From a house clearance in the north of England, with the maker's mark on the base" is a provenance. "From the 1950s, believed to be" without any evidence is a guess, and it should be labelled as one. If the item's history includes a previous owner, a previous use, or a previous repair, say so, because that history is part of what the buyer is buying. End with the practical details: how the item will be packed, how it will be sent, and what happens if it arrives damaged — because a fragile vintage item is only as safe as its packing, and a buyer who knows how it will be packed will buy with more confidence. Keep the description readable: short paragraphs, the important facts near the top, the full detail after them. A description that reads like an honest examination report will sell a vintage item far better than one that reads like an advertisement, because the vintage buyer is not buying a dream. They are buying an object with a past, and they want to know what that past was.

Photos: show the item the way a buyer would examine it

A vintage buyer examines an item the way a collector examines it: from the front, from the back, from the side, close up, and with attention to the details that prove what it is. The photo set should follow that examination. The first photo should show the item at its best, in good light, against a plain background, filling the frame — the photo that makes a browsing buyer stop. The photos after it should do the work of an examination: the item from the back and the side, the maker's mark or label in close-up, the details that show the era — the shape, the materials, the construction — and the condition, photographed honestly as the last section described. Include a photo that shows scale: the item beside something familiar, or held, or in a room, so the buyer can judge its size without relying on the measurements alone. For clothing and textiles, show the item flat and on a person or a stand, show the label, show the seams and the fastenings, and show any wear to the fabric. For jewellery, show the clasp, the back, and any marks or stamps, because those are the details a buyer will check first. For furniture, show the joints, the underside, and any repairs, because those are the details that tell a buyer how the piece was made and how it has been treated. Keep the light consistent across the set, and avoid filters that change the colour of the item, because a buyer who receives an item in a different colour from the photos will feel misled, however the filter was intended. If the item has a patina — the softening and darkening that age gives to brass, leather or wood — photograph it in light that shows it truly, and name it in the description, because patina is a selling point for the vintage buyer and a flaw for the buyer who wanted a new-looking item, and both buyers deserve to see it before they buy. Shop pages, sections and the vintage buyer's journey

A vintage shop is browsed differently from a new-goods shop. Vintage buyers browse by era, by style and by room, as well as by item, and the shop should make that browsing easy. Group the listings into sections by era or by style — 1950s, 1960s, 1970s, Art Deco, Mid-Century — as well as by item type, so a buyer who loves one era can see everything from it in one place. The shop announcement should say plainly that the shop sells genuine vintage items, what eras and kinds of items it specialises in, and how condition is described and photographed, because that announcement is the first thing a new visitor reads and it sets the expectation for every listing after it. A buyer who understands how a seller works will trust the listings more, and a vintage buyer who trusts a seller will come back when the next item from their favourite era arrives. Keep the shop consistent: the same honest condition language in every listing, the same photo style, the same measurement format, so that a buyer who has bought once knows what to expect next time. Mistakes that cost vintage sales

  • Calling a new item vintage — the buyer who wanted a genuine older item will never trust the shop again.
  • Guessing a decade without evidence — the listing is found by buyers who wanted a different era, and they leave.
  • Using every era word in the title — the title claims five eras and tells the buyer the seller knows none of them.
  • Hiding the wear — the careful buyer passes the listing by, and the buyer who buys it returns it.
  • Condition words with no detail — "good condition" tells the buyer nothing, and nothing is not enough for a one-of-a-kind item.
  • Missing or wrong measurements — the buyer cannot judge the item unseen, and a wrong measurement is the most common cause of a return.
  • Copying the same tags for every listing — the listings compete with each other instead of covering different era and item searches.
  • Filters that change the colour — the item arrives in a different colour from the photos, and the buyer feels misled.
  • Inventing a provenance — a romantic story with no evidence will be believed once and doubted ever after.
  • No photo of the maker's mark or the flaw — the two photos a serious buyer looks for first are the two that are missing.

Vintage selling on Etsy rewards the seller who treats every item as a small piece of history with evidence, rather than as stock to be shifted. Prove the age, name the era, write the title and tags from the evidence, describe the condition as you would want it described to you, measure twice, photograph the examination, and tell the true story in the description. Do that for every listing, and the shop will do what vintage shops do at their best: it will attract buyers who know what they are looking for, earn their trust with the first item, and keep them coming back for the next era, the next maker, and the next piece of history. Frequently asked questions

What counts as vintage on Etsy?
Etsy defines vintage items as items that are at least 20 years old. A vintage listing should be a genuine older item, not a new item made to look old, and not a handmade item in a vintage style. If you are unsure whether an item is old enough, check the evidence before you list it: a maker's mark, a label, a date stamp, a style detail, or a receipt or provenance you can describe honestly. When the age is uncertain, say so in the description rather than guessing a decade, because a buyer who discovers the age was overstated will feel misled.

Should I put the decade in my vintage listing title?
Yes, when you know it. The decade or era is one of the first things a vintage buyer searches for, because buyers usually start with an era — 1950s, 1970s, Art Deco, Mid-Century — and then narrow down to the item. Put the item first, then the era, so the listing is found for the item search and the era search at once. Only use a decade or era word you can support with evidence from the item itself. If the era is a guess, use a broader word such as vintage or retro in the title, and explain the uncertainty in the description.

How do I describe condition honestly for a vintage item?
Describe what you can see, in plain words, and photograph it. Name the wear, the marks, the repairs and the missing parts, and say where each one is on the item, so the buyer is not surprised when it arrives. Use plain condition words — excellent, good, fair — only alongside the detail that supports them, because a condition word on its own tells the buyer nothing. A description that names the flaws honestly will lose the buyers who wanted a perfect item, and it will keep the buyers who wanted a real vintage item with real history, which is exactly the buyer a vintage seller wants.

Can a vintage seller use the same tags for every listing?
No. Tags should describe the individual item, its era, its material, its style and its use, and those details are different for every listing. A tag set copied from one listing to another will describe the wrong item for most of the shop, and it will make the listings compete with each other for the same searches instead of covering different searches. Build each tag set from the item in front of you: the item words, the era words, the material and colour words, and the style and use words.
