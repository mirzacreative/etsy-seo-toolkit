Etsy SEO for Print-on-Demand Sellers

Updated 27 November 2026 · 12-minute read

Quick answer: Print-on-demand listings are found the same way other Etsy listings are found — by a title, tags and first photograph that match what a buyer types and what a buyer wants to see — but they fail in their own ways, and those ways decide whether a POD shop is found at all. The most common failure is sameness: the same design placed on many products, with the same title, the same tags and the same mockup style, so that no listing gives a buyer a reason to open it rather than another. The second failure is missing product information: a buyer who cannot tell what the product is made of, which sizes exist, or how to care for it will leave, however good the design is. Build each POD listing as its own listing, for the buyer who searches for that product: a title in that buyer's words, all thirteen tags filled with buyer phrases, mockups that show the product in use, and a description that answers the questions a buyer asks about that product. Use our titles, tags and descriptions guide, our 13-tags guide and our keyword research guide for the details, and treat the rest of this guide as the part that is specific to printed products.

What print on demand means for a listing

Print on demand is a way of selling a printed product without holding stock of it. The seller creates the design. A buyer orders the product. A production partner prints the design on the product and ships it to the buyer. The seller does not print the product, pack it, or keep a shelf of finished items waiting for orders. That arrangement has real advantages for a small shop: a seller can offer many designs and many products without paying for stock in advance, can test a new design without committing to a production run, and can stop selling a design that does not sell without being left with unsold items. It also has consequences for the listing that a seller who makes everything by hand does not face, and those consequences are the subject of this guide. The first consequence is distance. The seller does not handle the finished product before the buyer does, and in many cases has never held the exact product, in the exact size, with the exact design on it. The listing must therefore work harder to describe the product accurately, because the seller cannot rely on having seen and handled it. The material, the fit, the sizes, the colours and the care instructions must be stated from the information the production partner provides, checked where possible, and written in the listing so that the buyer knows what will arrive. The second consequence is sameness. Because the same design can be placed on many products with very little extra work, a POD shop can grow to dozens or hundreds of listings that differ only in the product, and sometimes not even in the title or the tags. That sameness is the shop's greatest SEO weakness, as the next sections explain. The third consequence is timing. A printed product takes time to produce after the order is placed, and then time to ship, and the buyer needs to know that before they buy. A listing that does not set out the production and delivery picture honestly will draw messages from buyers asking where their order is, and a shop that draws many such messages will spend its time answering them instead of building new listings. None of this makes print on demand a bad way to sell. It makes it a way of selling whose listings must be built with extra care, because the seller is asking a buyer to trust a product the seller has not held, described by photographs the seller did not take of the finished item, and delivered by a partner the buyer will never meet. The listings in this guide are built to earn that trust, one detail at a time.

How buyers search for printed products

A buyer searching for a printed product rarely searches for the production method. A buyer does not type a phrase about how a mug was made. A buyer types the product, the subject, the occasion, or the person. A buyer types the product and the design subject together: a mug with a particular animal on it, a sweatshirt with a particular phrase, a poster of a particular place. A buyer types the occasion: a gift for a birthday, a present for a teacher, a decoration for a new home, a shirt for a holiday. A buyer types the person: a gift for a father, a present for a friend who loves a particular hobby, a shirt for a team member. And a buyer types the detail that decides the purchase: the size, the colour, the material, or the style. A POD listing must be built for those searches, not for the seller's own vocabulary. The title should carry the product and the subject in the words a buyer would use, and the tags should carry the occasion, the person, and the detail. Our keyword research guide explains how to find the words buyers actually type, by looking at the search suggestions, at the words used in successful listings for similar products, and at the language buyers use in reviews and messages, and that method should be applied product by product for a POD shop. A design for a cat lover, for example, will be searched for differently on a mug, on a sweatshirt, and on a poster, because the buyer for each product is thinking about a different use, and the listing for each product should use the words of that use. The other thing to understand about how buyers search for printed products is that they compare. A buyer who searches for a printed mug will see many mugs, many of them with similar designs, and will choose on the evidence in front of them: the first photograph, the title, and the price as shown in search. A listing that looks like every other listing in that search will not be opened, however good the design is, because the buyer has no reason to prefer it. A listing that shows the product clearly, in use, with a title that names the subject and the use, will be opened, because it has given the buyer a reason. The work of POD SEO is therefore not only about being found. It is about being chosen, from among listings that look alike, by a buyer who is comparing quickly. Every section that follows — the title, the tags, the mockups, the description — should be judged by that test: does this listing give a buyer who is comparing a reason to choose it?

The sameness problem: one design, many listings, no reason to choose

The sameness problem is the central problem of print-on-demand SEO, and it deserves to be understood fully before any of the techniques, because every technique in this guide is, in one way or another, a way of solving it. The problem arises from the ease of the method. A seller creates a design, and within minutes can place it on a mug, a sweatshirt, a poster, a tote bag, a cushion and a phone case. Each placement becomes a listing. Each listing, if the seller is in a hurry, carries the same title, the same tags, the same description with only the product name changed, and mockups that differ only in the shape of the product. The shop now has six listings that say the same thing, show the same design in the same way, and give a buyer no reason to prefer one over another. Worse, the shop has six listings competing with each other for the same searches, because they all use the same words, and a buyer who searches for the design subject will meet the same listing six times in different shapes, and will conclude that the shop has one idea repeated rather than a range worth browsing. The fix is not to sell fewer products. It is to make each listing a listing in its own right. Each product has its own buyer, its own use, and its own questions, and the listing should be built for that buyer. The mug listing should be written for the buyer who wants a mug: the subject, the use, the size, the material, the care. The sweatshirt listing should be written for the buyer who wants a sweatshirt: the fit, the sizes, the material, the season. The poster listing should be written for the buyer who wants wall art: the sizes, the framing question, the room. The design is the same. The listing is not. The same applies within a single product. A design offered in several colours, or in several sizes, should not become several listings that differ only in the colour or size word in the title. The colours and sizes belong in the listing's variants, described once, clearly, in a single listing that the buyer can configure, unless there is a genuine reason — a different photograph, a different use, a different buyer — for a separate listing. A shop that does this will have fewer listings, each of them better, and each of them findable by the buyer it was built for. A shop that does the opposite will have many listings, each of them thin, and a buyer who meets three of them in one search will leave the shop rather than browse it. The rest of this guide shows, section by section, how to build the single, strong listing for each product.

Titles for POD listings: the product, the subject, and the use

A print-on-demand title should do three things in its first words: name the product, name the subject of the design, and hint at the use. A buyer scanning search results reads the first words of a title first, and often reads no further, so the most important words should come first and the least important words should come last. A title that begins with the seller's shop name, with a general word, or with a phrase about the production method wastes the words the buyer reads first. A title that begins with the product and the subject — the thing itself, and the design on it — gives the buyer what they came for at once. The title should use the words a buyer types, not the words the seller uses in the studio. A seller may think of a design by its file name, its series name, or its artistic style. A buyer thinks of it by its subject, its occasion, and its use, and the title should use the buyer's words. Our titles, tags and descriptions guide covers the general method in detail, including how to order the words, how to avoid repeating the same phrase, and how to write a title that reads as a sentence rather than a list of keywords, and that method applies in full to POD titles. Two points are specific to POD. The first is that the title must not promise what the product does not deliver. A title that names a material, a size, or a feature the product does not have will draw a buyer who will be disappointed, and a disappointed buyer costs the shop more than a buyer who never clicked. Check every descriptive word in the title against the product information from the production partner before publishing. The second is that the title must differ between products. A mug, a sweatshirt and a poster carrying the same design should not carry the same title with only the product word changed, because that is the sameness problem in its simplest form. Each title should be written for the buyer of that product, using the words of that product's use, so that a buyer searching for a mug meets a mug title and a buyer searching for wall art meets a wall-art title. A seller who writes each title separately, rather than copying one title across products, will spend more time on each listing and will have a shop in which every listing can be found by the buyer it was written for. That time is the real cost of POD SEO, and it is also the reason a careful POD shop can be found while a hurried one cannot.

Thirteen tags, built for the product in the listing

Tags are the phrases a listing can be found by that do not fit in the title, and a POD listing should use all thirteen of them, filled with phrases a buyer would type. The method is covered in full in our 13-tags guide, and the POD-specific point is about coverage: the thirteen tags for a mug listing should not be thirteen variations on the design subject. They should cover the four things a buyer searches by. First, the product: the product itself, its material or style, and the way it is used. Second, the design subject: the animal, the phrase, the place, the hobby, or the style that the design shows. Third, the occasion or use: the gift occasion, the season, the room, or the event the product is bought for. Fourth, the buyer or recipient: the person the product is bought for, described as a buyer would describe them. A tag set that covers all four will be found by buyers searching in any of those ways, while a tag set that only covers the subject will be found only by buyers who already know the subject. The tags should not repeat the title word for word, should not repeat each other, and should not contain phrases no buyer would search for, however clever they sound to the seller. A tag that no buyer types is a wasted tag, and a shop with many wasted tags is a shop that is hard to find. The tags should also be written per product, not copied across products. The tags for a sweatshirt should name the sweatshirt's world — the fit, the season, the layering, the person who wears it — and the tags for a poster should name the poster's world — the room, the wall, the framing, the size. Copying one tag set across every product carrying a design reproduces the sameness problem in the tags, and a buyer who searches for a poster will meet a listing whose tags are written for a mug. Write the tags after the title, for the product in front of you, using the keyword research for that product, and check them against the audit checklist in our shop audit checklist before publishing. A listing whose tags are careful, varied, and specific to the product will not guarantee that the listing is found, but it will give the listing every chance its title and photographs give it, and that is all any tag set can do.

Mockups and photographs: showing a printed product a buyer can trust

The first photograph of a POD listing does more work than any other part of the listing, because it is the part the buyer sees in search, at thumbnail size, beside many other listings. A POD seller faces a particular difficulty here: the seller usually does not have a photograph of the finished product, taken in good light, showing the exact design on the exact product. The seller has a mockup — a digital rendering of the design placed on a photograph or illustration of the product — supplied by the production partner or made with a mockup tool. A mockup can be a good first photograph, provided it is treated as a photograph that must earn trust, not as a placeholder that will do. The mockup should show the product clearly, at a size the buyer can read as a thumbnail, with the design large enough to be recognised and the product itself unmistakable. A mockup in which the design is small, the product is cropped, or the background distracts from the product will be passed over in search, however good the design is. The mockup should show the product in use where that is possible: a mug held or on a desk, a sweatshirt worn, a poster on a wall, a tote bag carried. A product shown in use answers the buyer's first unspoken question, which is what this thing will look like in my life, and a product shown floating on a plain background answers only the question of what the design looks like, which the buyer can see from the design alone. The listing should carry more than one image. The later images should show the product from other angles, show the detail of the print, show the range of colours or sizes where those matter, and show the product's scale — a mug beside an ordinary object, a poster on a wall with furniture in the frame, a sweatshirt's fit described in the image as well as in words. Where the production partner supplies lifestyle mockups, use the ones that look honest and well lit, and avoid the ones that look artificial, over-smoothed, or unlike any real room or real person. Where the seller can order a sample and photograph it, that photograph will usually be the strongest image in the listing, because it is evidence that the product exists as described, and a sample photograph should replace a mockup as the first image wherever it is available. Our listing photos guide covers the general principles — clarity at thumbnail size, honesty about the product, and the order in which images should appear — and those principles apply with extra force to POD, where the photograph is the only evidence the buyer has that the product will look as described. A listing whose first photograph is clear, honest, and specific to the product will be opened. A listing whose first photograph is a small design on a vague product shape will not, and no title or tag set will rescue it.

Descriptions that answer a POD buyer's questions

A POD description should answer, in order, the questions a buyer asks before buying a printed product they cannot hold. The first question is what the product is: the product, the material, and the way it is made, stated plainly. The second question is what the buyer will receive: the sizes, the colours, and any choices the buyer must make, stated so that the buyer can choose without guessing. The third question is how to look after it: the care instructions, stated as they apply to the printed product, not copied from a general guide. The fourth question is what happens after ordering: the production and delivery picture, stated honestly, including that the product is made after the order is placed where that is the case, so that the buyer is not surprised by a wait they did not expect. The fifth question is what the buyer may do with the design: the use the buyer is buying — a personal gift, a decoration for their own home — stated plainly, without promises the seller cannot keep. The description should be written for the product in the listing, not copied from another product's listing with the product name changed. A description copied across products will contain details that do not apply to the product in front of the buyer — a care instruction for a mug in a sweatshirt listing, a size note for a poster in a tote bag listing — and a buyer who spots one such detail will doubt the rest. The description should also be honest about what the seller does not know. Where a detail depends on the production partner — the exact shade of a colour, the exact feel of a material — the description should say what is known and should not invent what is not. A buyer who receives a product that matches its description will leave a review that helps the shop. A buyer who receives a product that does not match its description will leave a message, or a review, that costs the shop far more. The description is also the place for personalisation, where the listing offers it. If a buyer can add a name, a date, or a message, the description should say exactly what can be added, where it will appear, and what the buyer should write, with an example, so that the buyer does not have to guess. A personalised POD listing that explains personalisation clearly will draw fewer confused messages than one that mentions it in passing. Our titles, tags and descriptions guide covers the structure of a description that is scanned quickly and read fully when it matters, and our digital downloads guide is the companion for the different case of a file the buyer receives at once, where the questions are about the file, not the printed product.

Variants, sizes, colours and personalisation: one listing, clearly configured

Variants are the choices a buyer makes within a listing: the size, the colour, the style, or the personalisation. For a POD listing, variants are where the sameness problem is either solved or made worse. A listing that offers its sizes and colours as variants, in one listing, with a photograph for the main choices and a description that explains each choice, is a listing a buyer can configure without leaving the page. A shop that instead creates a separate listing for each size or colour will have many thin listings, each competing for the same searches, each with the same title and tags, and a buyer who finds three of them will be confused about which one to buy. The general rule is one listing per product, with the choices as variants, unless a choice changes the product so much that it is genuinely a different listing — a different photograph, a different use, or a different buyer. The variants should be named as a buyer would name them. A size variant should use the size name the buyer recognises, with the measurements stated in the description so that the buyer can check them. A colour variant should use the colour name the buyer will see, and the listing's photographs should show the main colours where a colour changes the look of the design. A personalisation variant should state exactly what the buyer should provide, and should make clear what happens if the buyer leaves the personalisation field empty. The price of each variant should be considered as part of the listing, not as an afterthought: our pricing strategy guide covers how to think about pricing a listing as a whole, including the costs and charges that apply to a sale, and the same thinking applies to POD, with the production cost of each variant taken into account. This guide states no figures for any charge, because charges can change and a figure copied from an older guide may be wrong; a seller should check the charges that apply to their own shop at the time of listing. The habit to build is to review the variants whenever a listing is reviewed: are all the sizes still available, are the colours still offered, does the personalisation still work as described, and does the description still match what the production partner supplies? A listing whose variants are out of date will draw messages from buyers who chose a variant that no longer exists, and those messages are a sign that the listing was not maintained. A listing whose variants are current, clear, and easy to choose from will convert the buyer who found it, which is the whole purpose of the listing.

Shop structure for a POD shop: sections, announcement and trust

A POD shop is judged as a shop, not only as a collection of listings, and the shop's structure should help a buyer who lands on one listing to browse the rest with confidence. Shop sections should be organised by the way buyers think, not by the way the seller files designs. A buyer thinks by product, by occasion, and by recipient, so sections named for products, for occasions, and for recipients will be useful, while sections named for design series, file names, or production partners will not. A shop with many designs on many products needs sections that keep the range navigable: a buyer who arrived for a mug should be able to find the mugs, and a buyer who arrived for a gift should be able to find the gifts, without scrolling through every listing the shop has ever made. The shop announcement should say, in a few lines, what the shop sells, how the products are made, and what a buyer should expect after ordering, including the production picture. It should not make promises the shop cannot keep, and it should not repeat the description of a single listing. The announcement is read by buyers who are deciding whether to trust the shop, and a plain, honest announcement will do more for that decision than a long one full of claims. The shop's policies, photographs and reviews complete the picture. A shop with no reviews yet should not invent trust: it should earn it listing by listing, by describing products accurately, by answering messages promptly, and by delivering what the listing promised. Our shop announcement guide and our new shops guide cover the shop-level work in detail, including how a new shop should be set up before its first listings go live, and this guide's advice should be read alongside them. The POD-specific point is disclosure. A buyer should be able to tell, from the shop and from the listing, that the product is printed after ordering by a production partner, and should not be led to believe that the seller printed it by hand in their own studio. Sellers should check Etsy's current policies on production partners and disclosure, and should describe the arrangement in the listing and the shop in the terms those policies require. Honesty here is not only a policy matter. It is a trust matter: a buyer who understands the arrangement before buying will wait patiently for a product made to order, while a buyer who discovers it afterwards will feel misled, however good the product is. A shop that is plain about how its products are made will attract the buyers who are happy with that arrangement, and those are the buyers who will leave the reviews the shop needs.

Launching a POD listing: a checklist before it goes live

A POD listing should be launched like a product, not published like a draft. The checklist below is the minimum a listing should pass before it goes live, and a seller who works through it for each listing will avoid most of the problems that POD shops complain about afterwards. First, the keyword check: the title uses the words buyers type for this product, found through the keyword research method, and the tags cover the product, the subject, the occasion and the recipient. Second, the title check: the title names the product and the subject in its first words, promises nothing the product does not deliver, and differs from the titles of the other products carrying the same design. Third, the photograph check: the first photograph reads clearly as a thumbnail, shows the product in use where possible, and shows the design large enough to be recognised, and the later photographs show the detail, the scale, and the main choices. Fourth, the description check: the description answers what the product is, what the buyer will receive, how to care for it, what happens after ordering, and what personalisation, if any, is offered. Fifth, the variant check: the sizes, colours and choices are current, named as a buyer would name them, and matched by the description and the photographs. Sixth, the policy check: the listing describes the production arrangement honestly, in line with Etsy's current policies as the seller has read them, and the shop announcement says the same thing. Seventh, the sample check: where a sample has been ordered, the listing has been checked against the sample — the colours, the size, the feel, the print quality — and any detail that does not match has been corrected. Where no sample has been ordered, the listing states only what the production partner's information supports. A listing that passes all seven checks is ready to go live. A listing that fails one should be fixed before it goes live, not after, because a listing that goes live with a weak title or a misleading photograph will be judged by buyers on that version, and a seller who fixes it a week later will have lost the buyers who saw the first version. Keep the checklist with the audit checklist, use it for every new listing, and use it again whenever a listing is renewed or edited, as our listing renewal guide explains for the review moment that renewal provides. Launching carefully is slower than publishing quickly, and it is the slower launch that builds a shop buyers return to.

Seasonal POD: planning printed gifts before buyers search

Printed products are often bought as gifts, and gift buying is seasonal. A POD seller who plans for the seasons will have listings live, in good shape, before buyers start searching, while a seller who reacts to a season will be publishing listings while the search is already under way, and will miss the buyers who searched early. The seasonal work has three parts. First, the calendar: the occasions that matter for the shop's products — the winter holidays, the occasions for family gifts, the seasons for outdoor products, the events for team and group products — noted in advance, with a date by which each season's listings must be live. Second, the listings: the seasonal words in the title and tags, the seasonal mockups where a seasonal photograph helps, and the description adjusted for the occasion, all prepared before the season, not during it. A listing that gains its seasonal words in the middle of the season has missed the early searchers, and a listing whose seasonal words remain after the season has ended will look neglected to the next buyer who finds it. Third, the production picture: the time a printed product takes to produce and deliver matters more at seasonal peaks, when both production and delivery can take longer, and the listing and the shop should set expectations honestly before the peak, not after the first delayed order. Our holiday listings guide covers the seasonal preparation in detail, and its advice should be applied to POD listings with the production picture in mind. The seasonal routine for a POD shop should also include an end-of-season review: which seasonal listings were found, which were bought, which drew messages about production or delivery times, and what should change for the next season. A shop that reviews its seasons will improve each year, because the same occasions return, and the listings that were built for them can be improved rather than rebuilt. A shop that does not review will repeat the same seasonal mistakes, and will publish the same listings, with the same weaknesses, for the same occasions, year after year. The seasons are the POD shop's best opportunity, because a printed gift is a natural gift, and a buyer searching for a gift is a buyer ready to buy. The shop that is ready before the buyer searches will be the shop the buyer finds.

Reviews, messages and repeat buyers for a POD shop

Reviews matter for a POD shop in a particular way, because the buyer is trusting a product they have not seen and a seller they have not met. A review from a buyer who received the product, who describes what arrived, and who says whether it matched the listing, is the evidence the next buyer uses to decide. A shop with a steady flow of such reviews will be chosen over a shop with no reviews, even where the designs are similar, because the reviews answer the question the mockups cannot: did the real product look like the picture, and did it arrive as described? The way to earn reviews is the same as for any shop: deliver what the listing promised, answer messages promptly and plainly, and ask, at the right moment, in a way that makes it easy. The right moment is after the buyer has received the product and had time to see it, not the moment the order is placed. The request should be short, honest, and easy to act on, and should never offer a reward, never ask only the happiest buyers, and never be written by the seller on the buyer's behalf. Messages deserve the same care. A POD shop will receive messages that other shops receive less often: questions about production times, about sizes, about colours, about whether a design can be placed on a different product, and about personalisation. Each message is a buyer deciding whether to trust the shop, and a prompt, specific answer will do more for the shop than any listing improvement. Where a message reveals a weakness in a listing — a size that was unclear, a colour that was not shown, a production time that was not stated — the listing should be fixed, so that the next buyer does not need to ask. Repeat buyers are earned the same way: by a product that matched its listing, by a message answered well, and by a shop that offers something new when the buyer returns. A buyer who bought a mug as a gift and returns for a sweatshirt for themselves is a buyer who trusted the shop once and is willing to trust it again, and the shop's sections, its new listings, and its honest descriptions should make that return easy. A shop that treats each sale as a single transaction will have to find every buyer again. A shop that treats each sale as the beginning of a relationship will find that buyers return, and that they bring others with them.

Mistakes that cost POD sellers views and sales

The mistakes worth naming are the ones POD sellers repeat without noticing, because the method makes them easy. Copying one title, one tag set and one description across every product carrying a design, so that no listing gives a buyer a reason to choose it. Using a first photograph in which the design is small, the product is unclear, or the background distracts, so that the listing is passed over in search. Describing a product from the production partner's information without checking it, so that the listing promises a material, a size, or a care instruction the product does not have. Creating a separate listing for each colour or size instead of one listing with clear variants, so that the shop fills with thin listings competing for the same searches. Leaving seasonal words in a listing after the season has ended, so that the shop looks neglected. Promising a production or delivery time the seller cannot keep, so that the buyer waits without news and messages the shop. Describing the production arrangement in a way that misleads the buyer about how the product was made, so that the buyer feels misled on arrival. Publishing a listing without a sample check where a sample was available, so that a colour or size surprise is discovered by the buyer rather than the seller. And judging a listing by a single week of views, rather than by the record over several weeks, and changing the title, tags and photographs all at once in response, so that no one can tell which change helped or hurt. Avoid those, build each listing for the buyer of that product, keep the photographs honest and the description complete, and review each listing when it is renewed, and the shop will be findable, trustworthy, and worth returning to.

Frequently asked questions

Is print on demand allowed on Etsy?
A seller should check Etsy's current seller policies before opening or running a print-on-demand shop, and should keep checking them, because the rules that govern production partners, handmade claims and what must be disclosed in a listing can change. This guide does not state the current policy as fact. The practical habit is to read the policy that applies to your own shop, to describe the production arrangement honestly in the listing and the shop, and to keep a record of where each product is made and who makes it, so that any question from a buyer can be answered plainly. If a listing or a shop practice does not fit the policy as written, change the listing or the practice before it becomes a problem.

Can I use the same design on many print-on-demand products?
You can, but each listing should still earn its place. A design that is placed on a mug, a sweatshirt, a poster and a tote bag without any change to the title, the tags, the mockups or the description will read as the same listing repeated, and a buyer who sees several of them will have no reason to choose one over another. The better habit is to treat each product as its own listing, built for the buyer who searches for that product: the title uses the product's own words, the tags name the product and its use, the mockups show that product in use, and the description answers the questions a buyer asks about that product. The design can be the same. The listing should not be.

How many tags should a print-on-demand listing use?
Use all thirteen tags, filled with phrases a buyer would type, as our 13-tags guide explains in detail. For a print-on-demand listing, the tags should cover the product, the design subject, the occasion or use, and the buyer: the product and its material or style, the subject of the design, the occasion it is bought for, and the person it is bought for. Do not fill tags with the same phrase repeated, with words already used in the title, or with phrases no buyer would search for. Tags are one part of the listing, not a substitute for a good title, a clear first photograph, or a description that answers the buyer's questions, and a listing with thirteen careful tags and a weak title will still struggle to be found and clicked.

Why is my print-on-demand listing not getting views?
The likeliest causes are the same ones that affect any listing: the title does not use the words buyers type, the tags are empty or repeat the title, the first photograph does not read clearly as a small thumbnail, or the listing sits in a category buyers do not look in. For print-on-demand listings there are two extra causes to check. The first is sameness: a listing that looks identical to many other listings for the same design gives a buyer no reason to open it. The second is missing product information: a buyer who cannot tell from the listing what the product is made of, what sizes are available, or how it should be cared for will leave without buying. Check the listing against the audit checklist, fix the title, tags and first photograph first, and add the product details a buyer needs before judging anything else.
