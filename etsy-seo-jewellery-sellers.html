What makes jewellery different to search and to sell

Jewellery is small, personal, and bought for reasons that are often emotional: a gift, a keepsake, a piece for a wedding or an anniversary, a piece that marks a person or a moment. A buyer searching for jewellery is therefore searching in two ways at once. The buyer searches for the thing — a necklace, a ring, a pair of earrings, a bracelet — and the buyer searches for the meaning — a gift for a mother, a present for a friend, a piece for a bride, a keepsake for a new baby. A listing that only names the thing will be found by buyers who already know the thing they want, and will be missed by buyers who know the occasion and the person but have not yet chosen the piece. A listing that only names the occasion will be found by buyers browsing for gifts, and will be missed by buyers who know the piece they want. A jewellery listing must be built for both kinds of search, and it must be built for a third difficulty that most other products do not face: scale. A necklace photographed on its own, without anything to show its size, could be any length. A ring photographed on its own could be any size. An earring photographed on its own could be light or heavy, small or large. The buyer cannot tell, from a single photograph, whether the piece will suit them, whether it will fit, or whether it will be comfortable to wear. The listing must answer those questions with photographs, measurements, and plain description, or the buyer will leave, however beautiful the piece is. The fourth difficulty is trust. A piece of jewellery is worn against the skin, given as a gift, and kept for years, and a buyer who buys it from a stranger online is trusting the seller's word about the material, the making, and the finish. A listing that states the material plainly, shows the finish close up, and explains the care honestly will earn that trust. A listing that is vague about the material, that hides the finish in a distant photograph, or that claims more than it can show will not, and the buyer who is misled once will not return. Everything in this guide follows from those four facts: the double search, the small scale, and the trust the buyer must place in words and photographs.

How jewellery buyers search: the piece, the material, the occasion, the person

A jewellery buyer rarely searches with a single word. The search is usually a combination: the piece and the material, the piece and the style, the piece and the occasion, or the piece and the person. A buyer types the piece with its material, because the material is part of what the buyer wants and part of what the buyer can afford. A buyer types the piece with its style, because the style — minimal, bold, delicate, vintage-inspired, modern — is the look the buyer has in mind. A buyer types the piece with the occasion, because the piece is being bought for a wedding, an anniversary, a birthday, a graduation, or a holiday, and the occasion decides what is suitable. And a buyer types the piece with the person, because the piece is being bought for a mother, a partner, a friend, a daughter, or for the buyer themselves, and the person decides the size, the style, and the price the buyer has in mind. A jewellery listing must be built for those combinations, not for the seller's own vocabulary. The seller may think of a piece by its collection name, its design number, or the name given to it in the studio. The buyer thinks of it by the piece, the material, the occasion, and the person, and the title and tags should use the buyer's words. Our keyword research guide explains how to find those words, by looking at the search suggestions, at the words used in listings for similar pieces, and at the language buyers use in reviews and messages, and that method should be applied piece by piece for a jewellery shop. A necklace and a pair of earrings in the same collection will be searched for differently, because the buyer for each piece is thinking about a different use, a different fit, and a different occasion, and the listing for each piece should use the words of that use. The other thing to understand is that jewellery buyers compare. A buyer who searches for a necklace will see many necklaces, many of them similar, and will choose on the evidence in front of them: the first photograph, the title, and the impression of quality and trust the listing gives. A listing that looks like every other listing in that search will not be opened, however fine the piece is, because the buyer has no reason to prefer it. A listing that shows the piece clearly, worn, with a title that names the piece, the material and the use, will be opened, because it has given the buyer a reason. Every section that follows should be judged by that test: does this listing give a buyer who is comparing a reason to choose it?

Titles for jewellery: the piece, the material, and the use, first

A jewellery title should do three things in its first words: name the piece, name the material, and hint at the use. A buyer scanning search results reads the first words of a title first, and often reads no further, so the most important words should come first and the least important words should come last. A title that begins with the seller's shop name, with a general word, or with a phrase about how the piece was made wastes the words the buyer reads first. A title that begins with the piece and the material — the thing itself, and what it is made from — gives the buyer what they came for at once. The title should use the words a buyer types, not the words the seller uses in the studio. A seller may think of a piece by its collection name or its design inspiration. A buyer thinks of it by its piece, its material, its style, and its use, and the title should use the buyer's words. Our titles, tags and descriptions guide covers the general method in detail, including how to order the words, how to avoid repeating the same phrase, and how to write a title that reads as a sentence rather than a list of keywords, and that method applies in full to jewellery titles. Two points are specific to jewellery. The first is that the title must not promise what the piece does not deliver. A title that names a material, a stone, a size, or a finish the piece does not have will draw a buyer who will be disappointed, and a disappointed buyer costs the shop more than a buyer who never clicked. Check every descriptive word in the title against the finished piece before publishing, and state the material exactly as it is. The second is that the title must differ between pieces. A necklace, a ring and a pair of earrings in the same collection should not carry the same title with only the piece word changed, because each piece has its own buyer, its own fit, and its own questions. Each title should be written for the buyer of that piece, using the words of that piece's use, so that a buyer searching for a ring meets a ring title and a buyer searching for earrings meets an earring title. A seller who writes each title separately will spend more time on each listing and will have a shop in which every listing can be found by the buyer it was written for.

Thirteen tags, built for the piece in the listing

Tags are the phrases a listing can be found by that do not fit in the title, and a jewellery listing should use all thirteen of them, filled with phrases a buyer would type. The method is covered in full in our 13-tags guide, and the jewellery-specific point is about coverage: the thirteen tags for a necklace listing should not be thirteen variations on the design subject. They should cover the four things a buyer searches by. First, the piece: the piece itself, its material and style, and the way it is worn. Second, the design subject: the motif, the stone, the shape, or the style that the piece shows. Third, the occasion or use: the gift occasion, the season, the wedding or celebration, or the everyday use the piece is bought for. Fourth, the buyer or recipient: the person the piece is bought for, described as a buyer would describe them. A tag set that covers all four will be found by buyers searching in any of those ways, while a tag set that only covers the subject will be found only by buyers who already know the subject. The tags should not repeat the title word for word, should not repeat each other, and should not contain phrases no buyer would search for, however clever they sound to the seller. A tag that no buyer types is a wasted tag, and a shop with many wasted tags is a shop that is hard to find. The tags should also be written per piece, not copied across pieces. The tags for a ring should name the ring's world — the size, the fit, the occasion, the person who wears it — and the tags for a pair of earrings should name the earrings' world — the fastening, the comfort, the occasion, the person who wears them. Copying one tag set across every piece in a collection reproduces, in the tags, the same sameness that copying one title reproduces in the titles, and a buyer who searches for earrings will meet a listing whose tags are written for a necklace. Write the tags after the title, for the piece in front of you, using the keyword research for that piece, and check them against the audit checklist in our shop audit checklist before publishing. A listing whose tags are careful, varied, and specific to the piece will not guarantee that the listing is found, but it will give the listing every chance its title and photographs give it, and that is all any tag set can do.

Photographs: showing scale, fit, detail and fastening

The first photograph of a jewellery listing does more work than any other part of the listing, because it is the part the buyer sees in search, at thumbnail size, beside many other listings. For jewellery, the first photograph has a particular job: it must show the piece clearly, at a size the buyer can read as a thumbnail, with the design large enough to be recognised and the piece itself unmistakable. A first photograph in which the piece is small, distant, or lost in a busy background will be passed over in search, however fine the piece is. The listing should then carry the photographs that answer, in order, the questions a buyer asks about a piece they cannot hold. The second photograph should show scale: the piece worn, or held, or beside an ordinary object, so that the buyer can judge its size. A necklace shown on a person shows its length. A ring shown on a hand shows its width. A pair of earrings shown worn shows how far they hang and how large they look on the ear. Without a scale photograph, the buyer must guess the size from the measurements alone, and many buyers will not guess: they will leave. The third photograph should show the detail close up: the finish, the setting, the clasp or fastening, and any detail that the buyer will notice when wearing the piece. A close view that shows the work honestly will earn trust, while a listing with no close view will leave the buyer wondering what the finish really looks like. The fourth photograph should show the fastening and the fit: how the piece closes, how it is put on, and how it sits when worn. A necklace whose clasp is unclear, a bracelet whose fastening is not shown, or a pair of earrings whose backs are not shown will draw messages from buyers asking how the piece works, and those messages are a sign that the photographs did not do their job. Later photographs can show the piece in use — worn with an outfit, given as a gift, worn for the occasion it was made for — and can show the packaging, where the piece is bought as a gift. Our listing photos guide covers the general principles — clarity at thumbnail size, honesty about the product, and the order in which images should appear — and those principles apply with extra force to jewellery, where the photograph is the only evidence the buyer has that the piece will look, fit, and feel as described. Where the seller can photograph the piece worn by a real person, in good light, that photograph will usually be the strongest in the listing, because it answers the size, fit, and wear questions at once. A listing whose photographs are clear, honest, and specific to the piece will be opened. A listing whose photographs show only the piece on its own, at a distance, will not, and no title or tag set will rescue it.

Materials, sizes and measurements: the detail buyers trust

A jewellery description must state, plainly and completely, what the piece is made from, how big it is, and how it fits, because those are the facts a buyer cannot check by holding the piece. The material should be stated exactly as it is, with the full detail a buyer needs: the metal, the stone or other material, any coating or finish, and any part of the piece that is made from a different material. A buyer who is buying a piece to wear every day, or to give as a gift, needs to know the material before buying, and a buyer who discovers after buying that the material is not what the listing suggested will feel misled, however fine the piece is. The description should not claim a benefit the piece cannot be shown to have. In particular, a seller should not claim that a piece suits sensitive skin, will not tarnish, will not fade, or will last for a stated time, unless the seller can support that claim from the material and the making, because a buyer who relies on such a claim and is disappointed will leave a review that costs the shop far more than the sale was worth. The measurements should be stated in the units buyers in the shop's markets use, for every dimension that matters: the length of a necklace, with the range where an adjustable chain is offered; the width and size of a ring, with the sizing method stated; the length and drop of a pair of earrings; the length and fit of a bracelet. The description should say how the buyer should measure, where the buyer must choose a size, and what to do if the buyer is between sizes, so that the buyer does not have to guess. The fit and fastening should be stated in the same plain way: how the piece closes, whether it is adjustable, and how it should be put on and taken off. A piece whose size or fastening is unclear will draw messages from buyers asking for the details the listing should have contained, and each such message is a buyer who was ready to buy and had to wait. Check the material, measurement, and fastening details against the finished piece before publishing, and check them again whenever a listing is renewed, as our listing renewal guide explains. A listing whose material and measurement details are complete, accurate, and easy to find will earn the trust that a jewellery sale needs, and will draw fewer messages, fewer returns, and better reviews than a listing that leaves the buyer to guess.

Descriptions that answer a jewellery buyer's questions

A jewellery description should answer, in order, the questions a buyer asks before buying a piece they cannot hold. The first question is what the piece is: the piece, the material, and the way it is made, stated plainly. The second question is how big it is and how it fits: the measurements, the size choices, and the fastening, stated so that the buyer can choose without guessing. The third question is how to look after it: the care instructions, stated as they apply to this piece and this material, not copied from a general guide. The fourth question is what happens after ordering: whether the piece is ready to send or made to order, and what the buyer should expect, stated honestly, so that a buyer who needs the piece for a particular date can decide in time. The fifth question is what personalisation, if any, is offered: what can be added, where it will appear, and what the buyer should write, stated with an example, so that the buyer does not have to guess. The description should be written for the piece in the listing, not copied from another piece's listing with the piece name changed. A description copied across pieces will contain details that do not apply to the piece in front of the buyer — a care instruction for a necklace in a ring listing, a size note for earrings in a bracelet listing — and a buyer who spots one such detail will doubt the rest. The description should also be honest about what the seller does not know. Where a detail depends on the material as supplied — the exact shade of a stone, the exact feel of a finish — the description should say what is known, including that natural materials vary, and should not invent what is not known. A buyer who receives a piece that matches its description will leave a review that helps the shop. A buyer who receives a piece that does not match its description will leave a message, or a review, that costs the shop far more. The description is also the place for the story of the piece, told briefly and honestly: what inspired it, how it is made, and what makes it different from a similar piece. A short, true story will help a buyer who is choosing a gift, because it gives the buyer something to say when the gift is given. A long, invented story will be recognised as invention, and will damage the trust the description was meant to build. Personalisation, gift messages and made-to-order pieces

Personalisation is common in jewellery, and it is also where listings most often fail the buyer, because the personalisation is mentioned without being explained. A listing that offers personalisation should state exactly what can be added — a name, initials, a date, a short message — where it will appear on the piece, how long it may be, and what the buyer should write in the personalisation field, with an example. It should state what happens if the buyer leaves the field empty, whether the piece will be made without personalisation or held until the buyer replies, and how the buyer should check the spelling before ordering, because a personalised piece that is made with a spelling the buyer did not intend cannot easily be remade. The listing should also state, honestly, whether the piece is ready to send or made to order, and how long the making will take, because a buyer who needs a piece for a wedding, an anniversary, or a birthday must know whether it will arrive in time, and a buyer who discovers after ordering that a piece is made to order will feel misled, however fine the piece is. Gift presentation deserves the same care. A piece bought as a gift is judged, in part, by how it arrives: the packaging, the gift message, and the absence of any price or seller information in the parcel, where the buyer has asked for it. The listing should say what packaging is included, whether a gift message can be added, and how the buyer should provide it, so that a buyer choosing a gift does not have to message the seller to ask. A made-to-order piece should also be described in a way that sets the right expectation: the piece will be made for the buyer, after the order, and may vary slightly from the photographs, because it is made by hand. That variation should be stated as a fact of handmade work, not hidden, because a buyer who understands it before buying will welcome a piece that is theirs alone, while a buyer who discovers it afterwards may read it as a fault. The habit to build is to review the personalisation and made-to-order details whenever a listing is reviewed: does the personalisation field still work as described, does the making time still hold, and does the description still match the piece as it is now made? A listing whose personalisation is clear, whose making time is honest, and whose gift presentation is stated will draw fewer confused messages than one that mentions personalisation in passing and leaves the buyer to guess the rest.

Shop structure for a jewellery shop: sections, announcement and trust

A jewellery shop is judged as a shop, not only as a collection of listings, and the shop's structure should help a buyer who lands on one listing to browse the rest with confidence. Shop sections should be organised by the way buyers think, not by the way the seller files designs. A buyer thinks by piece, by occasion, and by recipient, so sections named for pieces, for occasions, and for recipients will be useful, while sections named for collections, design numbers, or materials alone will be less useful to a buyer who does not yet know the collection. A shop with many pieces needs sections that keep the range navigable: a buyer who arrived for a necklace should be able to find the necklaces, and a buyer who arrived for a gift should be able to find the gifts, without scrolling through every listing the shop has ever made. The shop announcement should say, in a few lines, what the shop sells, how the pieces are made, and what a buyer should expect after ordering, including whether pieces are ready to send or made to order. It should not make promises the shop cannot keep, and it should not repeat the description of a single listing. The announcement is read by buyers who are deciding whether to trust the shop, and a plain, honest announcement will do more for that decision than a long one full of claims. The shop's policies, photographs and reviews complete the picture. A shop with no reviews yet should not invent trust: it should earn it listing by listing, by describing pieces accurately, by answering messages promptly, and by delivering what the listing promised. Our shop announcement guide covers the shop-level work in detail, and our new shops guide covers how a new shop should be set up before its first listings go live. The jewellery-specific point is care and honesty about materials. A buyer should be able to tell, from the shop and from the listing, what each piece is made from, how it should be cared for, and what will happen if the piece needs attention after it arrives, and should not be led to believe a piece is made from a material it is not. Honesty here is not only a policy matter. It is a trust matter: a buyer who understands the material and the care before buying will wear and keep the piece with confidence, while a buyer who discovers the truth afterwards will feel misled, however fine the piece is. A shop that is plain about its materials and its making will attract the buyers who are happy with them, and those are the buyers who will leave the reviews the shop needs.

Seasonal jewellery: gifts, weddings and occasions planned ahead

Jewellery is often bought as a gift, and gift buying is seasonal. A jewellery seller who plans for the seasons will have listings live, in good shape, before buyers start searching, while a seller who reacts to a season will be publishing listings while the search is already under way, and will miss the buyers who searched early. The seasonal work has three parts. First, the calendar: the occasions that matter for the shop's pieces — the winter holidays, weddings and anniversaries, birthdays, graduations, and the seasons for particular styles — noted in advance, with a date by which each season's listings must be live. Second, the listings: the seasonal words in the title and tags, the seasonal photographs where a seasonal photograph helps, and the description adjusted for the occasion, all prepared before the season, not during it. A listing that gains its seasonal words in the middle of the season has missed the early searchers, and a listing whose seasonal words remain after the season has ended will look neglected to the next buyer who finds it. Third, the making and delivery picture: where a piece is made to order, the time it takes to make matters more at seasonal peaks, when both making and delivery can take longer, and the listing and the shop should set expectations honestly before the peak, not after the first delayed order. Our holiday listings guide covers the seasonal preparation in detail, and its advice should be applied to jewellery listings with the making picture in mind. The seasonal routine for a jewellery shop should also include an end-of-season review: which seasonal listings were found, which were bought, which drew messages about making or delivery times, and what should change for the next season. A shop that reviews its seasons will improve each year, because the same occasions return, and the listings that were built for them can be improved rather than rebuilt. A shop that does not review will repeat the same seasonal mistakes, and will publish the same listings, with the same weaknesses, for the same occasions, year after year. The seasons are the jewellery shop's best opportunity, because a piece of jewellery is a natural gift, and a buyer searching for a gift is a buyer ready to buy. The shop that is ready before the buyer searches will be the shop the buyer finds.

Reviews, messages and repeat buyers for a jewellery shop

Reviews matter for a jewellery shop in a particular way, because the buyer is trusting a piece they have not held, from a seller they have not met, to be worn, given, or kept for years. A review from a buyer who received the piece, who describes what arrived, whether it matched the listing, how it fitted, and how it looked when worn, is the evidence the next buyer uses to decide. A shop with a steady flow of such reviews will be chosen over a shop with no reviews, even where the pieces are similar, because the reviews answer the question the photographs cannot: did the real piece look, fit, and feel like the listing said it would? The way to earn reviews is the same as for any shop: deliver what the listing promised, answer messages promptly and plainly, and ask, at the right moment, in a way that makes it easy. The right moment is after the buyer has received the piece and had time to wear it or to give it, not the moment the order is placed. The request should be short, honest, and easy to act on, and should never offer a reward, never ask only the happiest buyers, and never be written by the seller on the buyer's behalf. Messages deserve the same care. A jewellery shop will receive messages that other shops receive less often: questions about materials, about sizes, about fastenings, about whether a piece can be made in a different length or with a different stone, and about personalisation. Each message is a buyer deciding whether to trust the shop, and a prompt, specific answer will do more for the shop than any listing improvement. Where a message reveals a weakness in a listing — a size that was unclear, a fastening that was not shown, a material that was not stated — the listing should be fixed, so that the next buyer does not need to ask. Repeat buyers are earned the same way: by a piece that matched its listing, by a message answered well, and by a shop that offers something new when the buyer returns. A buyer who bought a necklace as a gift and returns for earrings for themselves is a buyer who trusted the shop once and is willing to trust it again, and the shop's sections, its new listings, and its honest descriptions should make that return easy. A shop that treats each sale as a single transaction will have to find every buyer again. A shop that treats each sale as the beginning of a relationship will find that buyers return, and that they bring others with them.

Mistakes that cost jewellery sellers views and sales

The mistakes worth naming are the ones jewellery sellers repeat without noticing, because the pieces are small and the listings are many. Copying one title, one tag set and one description across every piece in a collection, so that no listing gives a buyer a reason to choose it. Using a first photograph in which the piece is small, distant, or lost in a busy background, so that the listing is passed over in search. Showing the piece only on its own, without a worn photograph to show its scale, so that the buyer must guess its size. Describing the material vaguely, or claiming a benefit the piece cannot be shown to have, so that the buyer feels misled on arrival. Leaving the fastening unshown and the measurements unclear, so that the buyer must message the seller to ask how the piece closes and whether it will fit. Mentioning personalisation without explaining what can be added, where it will appear, and what the buyer should write, so that the buyer guesses and the piece is made wrong. Leaving seasonal words in a listing after the season has ended, so that the shop looks neglected. Promising a making or delivery time the seller cannot keep, so that the buyer waits without news and messages the shop. Publishing a listing without checking the material, measurement, and fastening details against the finished piece, so that an error is discovered by the buyer rather than the seller. And judging a listing by a single week of views, rather than by the record over several weeks, and changing the title, tags and photographs all at once in response, so that no one can tell which change helped or hurt. Avoid those, build each listing for the buyer of that piece, keep the photographs honest and the description complete, and review each listing when it is renewed, and the shop will be findable, trustworthy, and worth returning to. Frequently asked questions

Should a jewellery listing title name the material?
Yes, where the material is part of what the buyer is searching for and part of what the buyer needs to know before buying. A buyer searching for a necklace will often search with the material, the style, or the occasion included, and a title that names the piece, the material and the use in its first words will be recognised at once. Name only the material the piece is actually made from, stated as the seller can show it, and do not use a material word to describe a piece that is not made from that material. The description should then carry the full material detail — what the piece is made from, what any coating or finish is, and how it should be cared for — so that a buyer who opens the listing finds the detail the title promised.

How many photographs does a jewellery listing need?
A jewellery listing needs enough photographs for a buyer to judge the piece without holding it: the piece on its own, the piece worn or held so its size can be judged, a close view of the detail and the finish, and a view that shows how the piece fastens or closes. A single photograph, however beautiful, cannot answer the questions a buyer asks about size, fit, finish and fastening, and a buyer who cannot answer those questions from the photographs will leave without buying or will message the seller to ask. The first photograph should read clearly as a small thumbnail. The later photographs should answer, in order, the questions a buyer asks next: how big is it, how does it sit when worn, what does the detail look like close up, and how does it fasten?

Can I use the same title and tags for every jewellery listing?
No, and doing so is one of the commonest reasons a jewellery shop is hard to find. Each piece has its own buyer, its own use, and its own search words, and a title and tag set copied across necklaces, rings, earrings and bracelets will describe none of them well. A ring listing should be written for a buyer searching for a ring, with the ring's size, fit and fastening in mind, and an earring listing should be written for a buyer searching for earrings, with the earring's weight, fastening and comfort in mind. The method is the same for every listing, and it is covered in our titles, tags and descriptions guide and our 13-tags guide, but the words should be written fresh for each piece, from the keyword research for that piece.

How do I write a jewellery description that buyers trust?
State plainly what the piece is, what it is made from, its size and measurements, how it fastens, how it should be cared for, and what personalisation, if any, is offered, in that order, and state only what you can show to be true. A buyer who cannot hold a piece of jewellery trusts the description to stand in for holding it, and a description that is vague about the material, silent about the size, or unclear about the fastening will not earn that trust. Do not claim a benefit the piece cannot be shown to have, and do not promise a result — about skin, wear, or lasting — that you cannot support. A plain, complete description, checked against the finished piece, will earn more trust than a long description full of claims, and it will draw fewer messages from buyers asking for the details it should have contained.
