Etsy Listing Photos That Get Clicks

Updated 14 November 2026 · 8-minute read

Quick answer: Etsy buyers decide in seconds, and your photos decide for them. Every listing needs ten photos: a clean hero shot, a scale shot, a lifestyle shot, two or three detail close-ups, a how-to-use shot, a packaging shot, and a size comparison. Shoot them on a phone in soft natural light against a plain background, keep the product honest and in focus, and name your image files with real keywords before you upload. The order matters as much as the quality — the first photo earns the click, and the rest earn the sale. Why photos matter more than anything else on Etsy

On Etsy, the photo is the product until the parcel arrives. Buyers cannot pick your item up, turn it over, or try it on, so the listing photos do all of that work. A listing with one dark, blurry photo will lose to a similar listing with ten clear ones almost every time, even if the first product is better. Etsy search shows the first photo as a small square thumbnail, and buyers scroll past dozens of them in seconds. The photos also affect the sale after the click: listings where buyers can see the size, the materials, the details and the packaging get fewer questions, fewer surprises, and fewer returns. Good photos are not about expensive equipment. They are about planning the ten shots, using daylight well, and showing the buyer everything they would want to see if they were standing at your market stall.

The 10 photos every listing needs

Think of the ten photos as a fixed checklist you run through for every new listing. The order below is the order buyers should see them in.

1. The hero shot. The product alone, centred, filling most of the frame, on a plain background, well lit. This is the thumbnail buyers see in search, so it has to read clearly at a tiny size. No props, no clutter, no text on the image. If you only get one photo right, make it this one.

2. The scale shot. The product next to something everyone knows the size of — a hand, a coin, a mug, a book. Buyers are bad at guessing size from a photo alone, and "smaller than I expected" is one of the most common reasons for disappointment. Show the scale honestly.

3. The lifestyle shot. The product in use, in a real setting: a necklace worn, a mug on a desk, a print hanging on a wall, a bag carried on a shoulder. This is the photo that helps buyers imagine the product in their own life, and it is often the one that turns a browser into a buyer.

4. and 5. Detail close-ups. Two close shots of the things that make your product worth its price: the stitching, the clasp, the texture of the paper, the grain of the wood, the finish of the glaze. Shoot these from close up, in sharp focus, with the light falling across the surface so the texture shows. For handmade products, the details are the proof of the craft.

6. The how-to-use shot. Show the product doing its job: the planner open on a desk, the candle lit, the scarf styled two ways, the digital file on a tablet screen. Buyers who can see how something works are far more confident buying it.

7. The packaging shot. Show what arrives in the post — the box, the tissue, the thank-you card, the gift wrap if you offer it. This matters most for gifts: many Etsy buyers are buying for someone else, and the packaging photo tells them the gift will arrive looking like a gift.

8. The size comparison. A flat shot with a ruler or tape measure beside the product, or the product next to a standard object. This is different from the scale shot: it gives an exact measurement rather than a feeling. If your listing has sizes or variants, show the smallest and the largest side by side.

9. The back or second angle. The view buyers never think to ask for until after they buy: the back of a print, the underside of a mug, the reverse of a textile, the inside of a bag. A missing angle creates doubt, and doubt kills sales.

10. The process or materials shot. Your hands at work, the raw materials laid out, the tools on the bench. This photo tells the story of the product and supports the handmade claim in a way words cannot. It also gives buyers a reason to choose your listing over a mass-produced lookalike.

Phone photography tips that actually work

You do not need a camera to take listing photos that sell. A modern phone is more than enough if you use it properly. First, clean the lens — a smudged lens is the most common reason phone photos look soft. Second, turn off the flash and use natural light instead; flash flattens texture and creates harsh shadows. Third, tap the product on the screen to focus and lock the exposure, then check the photo at full size before moving on. Fourth, shoot slightly wider than you need and crop later — you can always crop in, but you cannot add back a cut-off edge. Fifth, take far more photos than you need: twenty shots per listing is normal, and you will pick the best ten. Sixth, keep the phone steady — rest it on a stack of books or a cheap tripod rather than holding it freehand. Finally, keep the editing light: a small brightness and contrast adjustment is fine, but heavy filters change the colours, and a buyer whose parcel arrives a different colour from the photo will not be happy.

Backgrounds and lighting

Background and light are the two decisions that make the biggest difference, and both are free. For the background, plain wins: a white or light-grey wall, a sheet of poster board, a linen cloth, or a plain wooden surface. The background should never compete with the product. If you sell colourful products, a plain background makes the colours pop; if you sell white or pale products, a darker background gives them definition. Keep one background consistent across your shop so listings look like they belong together. For lighting, soft daylight is the best free light there is. Shoot near a large window on a bright but cloudy day, or in open shade outdoors, with the light coming from the side rather than from behind you. Direct sunlight creates hard shadows and blown-out highlights, so avoid midday sun and move into the shade. If you shoot in the evening, use two lamps, one on each side, with white bulbs — never a single overhead light, which casts a shadow across the product. The test is simple: if you can see the product's true colour and texture in the photo, the light is right.

Image SEO: filenames and alt text

Photos help your listing get found, not just get clicked. Before you upload, rename each image file with real keywords: "handmade-ceramic-mug-blue.jpg" instead of "IMG_4821.jpg". Etsy reads filenames, and descriptive names help the listing show up in search, including image search. Keep the names short, use hyphens between words, and describe what is actually in the photo. When Etsy asks for alt text, write a short plain description of the image — what the product is, its colour and material, and what the photo shows — for example, "Handmade blue ceramic mug with speckled glaze, shown on a wooden table next to a book". Alt text helps buyers who use screen readers and gives search engines more text to understand the listing. It is a small step that takes a minute per photo and helps every listing a little. Mistakes that cost clicks

  • One photo only — buyers assume you are hiding something. Use all ten slots.
  • Text on the hero photo — sale banners and "20% off" text clutter the thumbnail and look unprofessional in search.
  • Dark, yellow or blurry photos — if the buyer cannot see the product clearly, they scroll past.
  • Stock photos or supplier images — buyers can tell, and on a handmade marketplace they expect to see the real product.
  • Inconsistent sizes or backgrounds — listings that look like they came from different shops feel untrustworthy.
  • Photos that do not match the product — a different colour, an old version, or a prop that is not included. Expect complaints and returns.
  • Forgetting the scale shot — "smaller than expected" is one of the most common reasons for a disappointing review.
  • Leaving filenames as IMG_4821.jpg — a minute of renaming is free search visibility you would otherwise throw away.

Your 30-minute photo routine

Set up once and the routine is fast. Put your plain background near the window, lay out the product, and work through the ten-shot checklist in order. Shoot extra frames of each one and pick the best. Rename the files with keywords, write a short alt text for each, and upload in the checklist order. Then open the listing on your phone and look at it the way a buyer would: does the first photo stop the scroll, does the scale make sense, can you see the details? If anything is unclear, reshoot that one photo — it takes two minutes and it is the difference between a listing that gets clicked and one that gets skipped. Frequently asked questions

How many photos should an Etsy listing have?
Use all ten photo slots. Each photo answers a different question — what it looks like, how big it is, how it is used, what it is made of — and listings with a full set of photos get more clicks and fewer questions from buyers than listings with one or two.

Can I take good Etsy photos with just a phone?
Yes. A modern phone is more than enough. Clean the lens, use soft natural light from a window, keep the background plain, tap to focus on the product, and take plenty of shots. Light and background matter far more than the camera you use.

What size should Etsy listing photos be?
Etsy displays the first photo as a square thumbnail, so compose your hero shot to work as a square and check how it looks cropped. Upload high-resolution images — at least 2000 pixels on the longest side is a good target — so buyers can zoom in on the details.

Does alt text help Etsy SEO?
Yes, in a small but real way. Alt text describes the image for screen readers and gives search engines more text about the listing. Write a short, honest description of what the photo shows, using the product's real keywords, rather than stuffing it with unrelated search terms.
