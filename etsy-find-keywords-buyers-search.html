How to Find Etsy Keywords Buyers Actually Search

Updated 20 November 2026 · 9-minute read

Quick answer: The best Etsy keywords come from buyers themselves, not from guessing. Start with Etsy's own search bar: type the first words of your product and read the autocomplete suggestions — those are real phrases buyers have typed. Then study the top-selling listings for those phrases and note the words in their titles and tags. Check your own shop's search terms in Etsy Stats to see what already brings people to you. Add seasonal phrases before the holidays they belong to, and keep a simple keyword list per product so each listing targets its own main phrase instead of competing with your other listings. Once you have the keywords, the next step is using them — our guide to Etsy titles, tags and descriptions shows how to write them into a listing, and our guide to filling your 13 tags shows how to use every tag slot.

Why most Etsy keyword guesses fail

Most sellers choose keywords by describing their own product — and that is exactly where it goes wrong. A seller writes "handcrafted artisan ceramic vessel" because that is how they think about their work, but the buyer types "handmade ceramic mug" because that is how they think about buying one. The listing and the buyer are speaking two different languages, and Etsy search can only match them if the listing uses the buyer's words. The gap is wider than sellers expect, because makers know the materials, the techniques and the craft terms, while buyers know the product, the occasion and the problem they want to solve. A buyer looking for a gift does not search for "stoneware" — they search for "birthday gift for mum" or "personalised mug for dad". A buyer looking for a wedding present does not search for the technique — they search for the occasion and the person. The first rule of Etsy keyword research is therefore to stop writing from your side of the counter and start writing from the buyer's side: what would a person who has never heard of your shop type into the search bar, on their phone, in a hurry, to find a product like yours? Every method in this guide answers that question from evidence — from what buyers actually typed — instead of from imagination. And the evidence is free: Etsy shows it to you in its own search bar, in your competitors' listings, and in your own shop statistics.

Method 1: Etsy's search-bar autocomplete

The fastest and most reliable keyword source is the one Etsy gives you for free: the search bar itself. Open Etsy in a private browser window, so your own search history does not shape the suggestions, and type the first word or two of your product — "ceramic mug", "leather journal", "baby blanket" — then stop and read the suggestions that drop down. Those suggestions are not random: they are phrases buyers have actually typed, often enough that Etsy offers them back. Each suggestion is a keyword worth writing down. Now type the phrase with a letter after it — "ceramic mug f", "ceramic mug g" — and read the new suggestions, because each letter opens a different branch of phrases. Then try the phrase with a word after it — "ceramic mug for", "ceramic mug with" — and read those too, because the words buyers add after a product name are usually the occasion, the person, or the feature: "for mum", "for coffee", "with handle", "for dad". Work through the alphabet for your main product words, and through the common endings — "for", "with", "in", "personalised", "handmade" — and you will have a long list of real buyer phrases in under an hour. Two things to note while you do it. First, the order of the suggestions is a rough signal of popularity: the phrases Etsy offers first are the ones buyers type most. Second, do this for each product separately — a mug and a vase are different products with different buyer phrases, even when they are made from the same clay. Keep the list in a simple document, one product per section, with the phrases grouped by type: product words, materials, occasions, people, colours and styles.

Method 2: Read your competitors' listings

The listings already ranking at the top of Etsy search for your main phrases are a second source of keywords — and they show you the vocabulary that is actually working. Search for your main phrase and open the first handful of listings, the ones with many sales and reviews. Read their titles carefully and write down the words they use that you do not: the materials they name, the occasions they mention, the styles they describe. Then look at their tags. Etsy does not show tags on the listing page itself, but the words in the title, the description's first lines, and the attributes the seller selected — colour, material, occasion, style — all reveal the keywords the listing is built around. You are not copying their listing; you are learning the language of the market. Notice what the top listings have in common and what they leave out: if every top mug listing mentions "handmade", "stoneware" and "gift for her", those words belong in your keyword list — and if none of them mentions a word you were planning to use, ask yourself whether buyers actually search for it. Pay attention to the listings with the most sales, not just the ones at the top of the page, because a listing with thousands of sales has been found by buyers many times, and its words are proven. And look at the listings just below the top too: the second page often holds newer shops that are ranking well with fresh phrasing, and their words can show you a gap the big sellers have missed. One caution: never copy another seller's title or description word for word. Use the research to learn the vocabulary, then write your own listing in your own words — that is also what our guide to titles, tags and descriptions covers, once your keyword list is ready.

Method 3: Your own shop's search terms

Once your shop has any traffic at all, your own statistics become the most honest keyword source you own — because they show the exact words that already brought real buyers to your listings. In your shop manager, open Etsy Stats and look at the search terms: the phrases buyers typed before they found you. Some of them will be phrases you already target, which tells you those keywords are working. Others will surprise you: a phrase you never thought of, bringing visitors to a listing you wrote for a different phrase. Those surprises are gold — they are buyer language you did not know existed, and they deserve a place in that listing's title or tags, or in a new listing built around them. Also look at which search terms bring visitors who actually buy, not just visitors who browse: a phrase that brings a hundred visitors and no sales is less valuable than a phrase that brings ten visitors and two sales. Check the search terms every month, because they change as your listings rank for new phrases and as the seasons change. And use the same statistics to find the gaps: a listing that gets views but few sales for its main phrase may be using the wrong main phrase — the buyers who find it wanted something slightly different from what the listing offers, and the right fix is a different keyword, not a different photo. Your own search terms are the one keyword source your competitors cannot see, which is exactly why they are worth checking regularly.

Method 4: Seasonal trends and occasions

Etsy is a seasonal marketplace, and the keywords buyers search for change with the calendar — which means your keyword research has to change with it too. A phrase that is quiet in July can be the busiest search on Etsy in November, and a listing that is optimised for the wrong season will be invisible when it matters most. The rule is to research seasonal keywords six to eight weeks before the season starts, because listings take time to climb in search, and a listing optimised for Christmas in December is too late. Think through the calendar for your products: Christmas, Mother's Day, Father's Day, Valentine's Day, weddings in spring and summer, back to school in late summer, Halloween in autumn. For each one, ask what buyers search for: the occasion plus the product, the occasion plus the person, and the occasion plus the word "gift" — "Christmas gift for mum", "personalised Mother's Day gift", "wedding gift for the couple". Use the search-bar autocomplete again for each occasion phrase, because the suggestions will show you the exact wording buyers use for that season. Then check your own shop's statistics from the same season last year, if you have them, to see which phrases brought buyers then. Add the seasonal phrases to your listings a few weeks before the season, in the tags first — the tags are the easiest place to swap a phrase in and out — and keep the main title focused on the product itself, so the listing still makes sense after the season ends. Seasonal keywords are how a small shop wins a share of the busiest searches on Etsy without competing head-on with the biggest sellers all year round.

Method 5: Keyword tools — what they add, and when to pay

There are tools built specifically for Etsy keyword research, and they do add something the free methods do not: they show search volumes, compare many phrases at once, and track how a listing's ranking changes over time. A tool can tell you, for example, that "personalised leather journal" is searched more often than "handmade leather notebook", or that a phrase is rising while another is falling — information that would take hours to gather by hand. Most of these tools offer a free tier with limited searches, and paid plans with more searches and more features. For a shop with a handful of listings, the free methods in this guide — autocomplete, competitor listings, your own search terms — give you the same raw material, and the money is better spent on the listings themselves. A tool becomes worth considering when you have many listings and want to compare dozens of phrases quickly, or when you want to track rankings week by week across the whole shop. Even then, treat the tool's suggestions as a starting point, not a verdict: a phrase with a high search volume is useless if it does not describe your product, and a phrase the tool marks as competitive can still be worth targeting if your listing is a better match for it than the listings currently ranking. The tool does not know your product; you do. Use it to find phrases you missed, then judge each one the same way you judge the free evidence — does a real buyer type this, looking for a product like mine?

Building your keyword list — and using it

Research is only useful if it ends in a list you can actually use, so the last step is to organise what you found. For each product, keep a simple document with four groups: the main phrase — the one exact phrase a buyer would type to find this product, which will go at the front of the title; the supporting phrases — materials, occasions, colours, sizes, styles, and who the product is for — which will fill the rest of the title and the tags; the seasonal phrases — the occasion words to swap in before a holiday; and the phrases to avoid — words that sound right but buyers do not search for, or words that describe a different product and would bring the wrong visitors. Then give each listing its own main phrase. If every listing uses the same keywords, your listings compete with each other for the same searches instead of covering different ones: one listing might target "personalised leather journal", another "handmade leather notebook", and a third "leather travel diary", so your shop as a whole covers far more searches than any single listing could. Mistakes that waste your keyword research

  • Guessing from your own vocabulary — makers describe techniques; buyers describe products, occasions and people. Research the buyer's words, not yours.
  • Using one-word keywords — "mug" or "journal" is too broad; buyers search in phrases, and a phrase tells Etsy exactly what the listing is.
  • Copying a competitor's title word for word — learn the vocabulary, then write your own listing. Copied listings do not rank better, and they are not yours.
  • Giving every listing the same main phrase — your listings then compete with each other instead of covering different searches.
  • Targeting a phrase that does not describe your product — it brings visitors who leave, and visitors who leave do not buy.
  • Researching Christmas keywords in December — seasonal phrases need to be in place six to eight weeks before the season starts.
  • Trusting a tool's volume number over your judgement — a high-volume phrase is useless if it does not describe what you sell.
  • Never checking your own search terms — your shop's statistics show the exact words that already bring buyers, and they change every month.
  • Stuffing the same phrase into the title, the tags and the description — repeating one phrase everywhere wastes the slots that could carry different phrases.

Frequently asked questions

How do I find the keywords buyers search for on Etsy?
Start with Etsy's own search bar: type the first words of your product and read the autocomplete suggestions — those are real phrases buyers have typed. Then study the top listings for those phrases: the words in their titles and the tags they use show you the vocabulary buyers and sellers both use. Check your own shop's search terms in Etsy Stats to see what already brings people to you, and look at seasonal trends before a holiday so your keywords match what buyers are searching for right now.

Are Etsy keyword tools worth paying for?
For most small shops, no — not at the start. Etsy's search-bar autocomplete, your competitors' listings, and your own shop's search terms give you the same raw material for free. A paid tool becomes useful when you have many listings and want to compare search volumes across dozens of phrases at once, or track how your rankings change over time. Even then, the tool is only as good as the judgement you apply to its suggestions — a phrase with a high search volume is useless if it does not describe your product.

Should I use the same keywords in every listing?
No. If every listing uses the same keywords, your listings compete with each other for the same searches instead of covering different ones. Give each listing its own main phrase — the most specific words for that product — and use different supporting phrases around it. One listing might target "personalised leather journal", another "handmade leather notebook", and a third "leather travel diary". Each one can then rank for its own search, and your shop as a whole covers far more of them.

How many keywords does one Etsy listing need?
One listing needs one main keyword phrase for the title, plus the supporting phrases that fill the rest of the title and all thirteen tags. Think of it as one main phrase — the exact words a buyer would type to find this product — and a dozen related phrases around it: materials, occasions, colours, sizes, styles, and who the product is for. The title carries the main phrase, and the tags carry the rest, so a buyer searching for any of them can find the listing.
