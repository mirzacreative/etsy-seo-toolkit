Etsy Ads: Are Promoted Listings Worth It?

Updated 19 November 2026 · 9-minute read

Quick answer: Etsy Ads — also called Promoted Listings — can be worth it, but only for listings that are already good enough to sell. They work by putting your listings higher in Etsy search results and on category pages, and you pay per click, with a daily budget you set yourself. For a new shop they can buy the first views and first sales that help listings rank on their own — but only if the photos, titles and tags are already strong. For an established shop they are best used to push proven sellers, seasonal lines and new ranges that need a first push. The way to judge them is simple: compare the ad spend with the revenue from orders that came from ad clicks over at least two weeks. If the revenue is comfortably higher than the spend, keep going. If it is not, pause the ads and fix the listings first — a bigger budget on a listing that does not convert only loses money faster.

What Etsy Ads actually are

Etsy Ads, still widely known as Promoted Listings, are Etsy's own advertising system inside the marketplace. When you turn them on, Etsy shows your chosen listings in prominent positions in Etsy search results and on category and browse pages, marked as ads. A buyer searching for a handmade ceramic mug might see your promoted mug near the top of the results, ahead of listings that are not being advertised — even listings from shops with more sales than yours. That placement is the whole product: you are paying for visibility, not for a sale. Whether the buyer then clicks, and whether they then buy, is down to your listing. Etsy decides which of your promoted listings to show for each search, based on how relevant the listing is to the search and on how your budget is set. You cannot choose the exact search terms your ads appear for in the way you can on a search engine — Etsy matches your listings to searches using the same words it uses for organic ranking: your titles, your tags, your attributes and your descriptions. That is why the quality of the listing matters so much before you spend a penny on ads: the ad system is, in effect, amplifying whatever your listing already says. A listing with clear product words will be shown to the right searches; a listing with vague words will be shown to the wrong ones, and you will pay for clicks from people who were looking for something else.

How they work, step by step

Setting up Etsy Ads takes a few minutes, and that simplicity is part of the risk — it is easy to start spending before the shop is ready. You open the advertising section of your shop manager, set a daily budget — the most you are willing to spend on ads in a day — and choose which listings to promote. You can promote all of your listings, or a selection. Etsy then enters your promoted listings into an auction for the searches they match: when a buyer searches, Etsy weighs the relevance of the competing listings and the budget behind each one, and shows the winners. You are charged when a buyer clicks a promoted listing, not when it is merely shown — so a listing that is shown a hundred times and clicked once costs you one click, while a listing shown ten times and clicked five times costs you five. The cost of each click varies: popular searches with many advertisers cost more per click than quieter ones, and the price moves with demand. Your daily budget is a cap, not a target: Etsy will not spend more than it in a day, but it may spend less on a quiet day, and the budget is shared across all of your promoted listings. You can change the budget at any time, pause individual listings, or turn the whole campaign off. The dashboard shows you, per listing, how many clicks it received, how much those clicks cost, and how many orders came from them — and those three numbers, read together, are the whole basis for deciding whether the ads are worth it.

What they cost — and what controls you actually have

With Etsy Ads you pay per click, and you control the daily budget — those are the two cost facts that matter, and everything else follows from them. There is no fixed price per click: it depends on the search, the competition for it, and the time of year. A click in a busy category at Christmas costs more than a click in a quiet category in February. Because the price varies, the honest way to think about cost is not "how much does a click cost?" but "how much does a sale cost?" — and that depends on your conversion rate, the share of clicks that become orders. A listing that turns one click in twenty into a sale can afford a much higher cost per click than a listing that turns one in two hundred into a sale. That is why two shops can spend the same daily budget and get very different results. Your controls are the daily budget, which listings are promoted, and whether the campaign is on at all. You cannot set a maximum cost per click, and you cannot choose the exact searches your ads appear for. That limited control is a reason to start small: a modest daily budget that you can afford to run for two or three weeks while you learn which listings earn their clicks back and which do not. Raise the budget only on the listings that are already turning clicks into sales. And remember that ad spend is on top of everything else Etsy charges on an order — the ad cost is an extra cost of getting the sale, so it has to be judged against the profit on the order, not just the order value.

When Etsy Ads help a new shop

A new shop has a specific problem that ads can solve: nobody can find it. A listing with no sales history, no reviews and little search history sits low in organic results, because Etsy has no evidence yet that buyers like it. Ads can buy that first evidence. A small daily budget on your two or three strongest listings can bring the first views, and if the listings are good, the first sales — and those sales and reviews then help the listings rank on their own, so you need the ads less over time. That is the best case, and it is real. But it only works if the shop is ready. Before you turn ads on, check the basics: clear photos on a plain background that show the product properly, a title that names the product, material and occasion in the words buyers search for, all thirteen tags filled with real search terms, and a price that makes sense against similar listings. If you are setting up from scratch, our Etsy SEO guide for new shops walks through the launch in order, and our guide to Etsy titles, tags and descriptions shows how to write the words the ads will be matched on. If any of those basics are weak, fix them before spending: an ad can bring a buyer to a listing, but it cannot make them buy a product they cannot see properly or a listing they do not trust. For a new shop, the other rule is to promote few listings, not all of them. Pick your best two or three — the products with the clearest photos, the most obvious buyer, and a price that leaves room for the ad cost — and give them a small budget for two weeks. Promoting everything at once spreads the budget so thin that no listing gets enough clicks to learn from, and it spends money on your weakest products too.

When they help an established shop

For a shop that already sells, ads do a different job: they push what is already working, and they give new products a first push. The best use of ads in an established shop is to promote your proven sellers — the listings with good photos, good reviews and a steady conversion rate — so they appear higher in search during the periods when it matters most: the run-up to Christmas, Mother's Day, wedding season, or any peak for your products. A proven listing that already converts well is the safest place to spend, because you know from its history what a click is worth. Ads are also useful for launching a new range: a new listing has no sales history, so a short, small-budget push can bring it its first orders and reviews faster than waiting for organic ranking to catch up. And they can help a listing that is good but buried — a strong product that sits on page three of search for its main term, where few buyers look. What ads should not do in an established shop is prop up a listing that does not sell on its own. If a listing gets clicks from ads and almost never converts, the problem is the listing — the photos, the price, the description, or the product itself — and no amount of ad spend fixes it. Check the dashboard per listing, not just the campaign total: the campaign can look healthy while half the budget is being spent on listings that never sell. Pause those listings, and move the budget to the ones that do.

How to judge ROI honestly

The return on Etsy Ads is not a feeling — it is a comparison you can do with three numbers from your dashboard, over the same period: ad spend, orders from ad clicks, and the revenue from those orders. Wait at least two weeks before judging, and a full month is better, because a week of data on a small budget is mostly noise: one order can make a bad week look good, and one quiet weekend can make a good week look bad. Do the sums per listing, not just for the campaign, because one strong listing can hide three weak ones. For each listing, ask: how much did its clicks cost, and how much revenue did the orders from those clicks bring? Then compare the revenue with the spend — and remember that the revenue is not profit. The ad cost is an extra cost on top of the product cost, the packaging, and Etsy's other charges on the order, so the ad-driven revenue needs to be comfortably higher than the ad spend, not just slightly higher, for the ads to be paying for themselves. A useful habit is to work out, for each listing, the most you can afford to pay for one order — the profit on the order, minus a margin you want to keep — and then check the dashboard: how many clicks does that listing need, on average, to get one order, and what do those clicks cost? If the cost of the clicks for one order is below what you can afford, the ads work for that listing. If it is above, they do not, and the fix is either a better-converting listing or no ads for it. Run the numbers monthly. A listing that was profitable in November may not be in February, when the cost per click and the conversion rate both change with the season.

When NOT to run Etsy Ads

There are times when the right decision is not to advertise at all. Do not run ads on a listing with weak photos — a buyer who clicks and sees a dark, blurry or cluttered photo will leave, and you will have paid for that click. Do not run ads on a listing whose title and tags are vague, because Etsy will show it to the wrong searches and you will pay for clicks from people who wanted something else. Do not run ads when your prices leave no room for the ad cost: if the profit on an order is small, a handful of clicks can eat it entirely, and every sale the ads bring actually loses money. Do not run ads to fix a shop with no reviews and no trust signals if the listings themselves are not ready — get the basics right first, using our Etsy shop SEO audit checklist to check the whole shop. Do not run ads during a period when you cannot fulfil orders quickly — a holiday, a backlog, a holiday closure — because the sales the ads bring will arrive late and earn bad reviews. And do not run ads as a habit without checking them: an ad campaign left running for months without review can quietly spend money on listings that stopped converting long ago. If any of these apply, pause the ads, fix the underlying problem, and come back to advertising when the shop is ready for it. Not advertising is not losing — it is refusing to pay for clicks that cannot become profitable sales.

A simple 30-day test plan

If you want a straight answer to "are Etsy Ads worth it for my shop?", run a small, honest test instead of guessing. Week one: fix the basics first — photos, titles, all thirteen tags filled — using our guide to filling your 13 tags, and pick your two or three strongest listings. Week two: turn ads on for those listings only, with a small daily budget you can afford to lose, and change nothing else about the shop. Week three: leave it alone and let the data build up — do not raise the budget because of one good day, and do not panic over one bad one. Week four: read the dashboard per listing. For each listing, compare the ad spend with the revenue from orders that came from ad clicks, and work out the cost of one order from ads. Keep the ads on the listings where the cost of an order is comfortably below the profit on that order; pause them on the listings where it is not. Then repeat the test with the next product, or raise the budget slowly on the winners — a little at a time, checking the numbers each week. And keep improving the listings themselves while the test runs: better photos and clearer titles lift the conversion rate, and a better conversion rate makes the same ad spend profitable where it was not before. That is the whole method: small budget, few listings, two weeks of patience, and a decision made from the numbers — not from the feeling that advertising must be helping because the views went up.

Mistakes that waste money on Etsy Ads

  • Advertising before the listings are ready — weak photos or vague titles turn paid clicks into bounces.
  • Promoting every listing at once — the budget spreads so thin that no listing gets enough clicks to learn from.
  • Judging after three days — a few days of data is noise; wait at least two weeks.
  • Reading the campaign total only — one strong listing can hide three weak ones burning the budget.
  • Comparing ad revenue with ad spend and forgetting profit — the ad cost sits on top of product cost and Etsy's other charges.
  • Raising the budget on a listing that gets clicks but no orders — more budget on a listing that does not convert only loses money faster.
  • Leaving ads running for months without review — listings that stopped converting keep spending quietly.
  • Advertising when you cannot fulfil orders — late deliveries from ad-driven sales earn bad reviews.

Frequently asked questions

How do Etsy Ads (Promoted Listings) work?
Etsy Ads boost your listings in Etsy search results and on category pages, marked as ads. You set a daily budget and choose which listings to promote; you are charged per click, not per view, so you only pay when a buyer clicks a promoted listing. Etsy decides which of your promoted listings to show for each search, based on the listing's relevance and your budget.

Are Etsy Ads worth it for a new shop?
They can be, but only after the basics are in place. A new shop with strong photos, clear titles and full tags can use a small daily budget to get its first views and its first sales, which then help the listings rank on their own. A new shop that advertises listings with weak photos or vague titles will pay for clicks that do not turn into sales — and that money is wasted. Fix the listings first, then advertise.

How do I know if my Etsy Ads are making money?
Compare what the ads cost with what they earned. In your Etsy Ads dashboard, check the ad spend against the revenue from orders that came from ad clicks, over the same period — at least two weeks. If the revenue from ad-driven orders is comfortably higher than the ad spend after Etsy's other fees, the ads are paying for themselves. If the spend is higher than the revenue it brought in, pause the ads and fix the listings first.

How much should I spend on Etsy Ads per day?
Start small — a modest daily budget that you can afford to lose for two or three weeks while you learn what works. There is no minimum that guarantees results, and a large budget on unproven listings only loses money faster. Raise the budget only on the listings that are already turning clicks into sales; keep the budget low, or pause it, on listings that get clicks but no orders.
