Etsy Pricing Strategy: Price Handmade Items for Profit

Updated 24 November 2026 · 11-minute read

Quick answer: A handmade item is priced for profit when its price covers four things and leaves a fifth: the materials in that exact item, the maker's time at a fair hourly rate, the share of the running costs that every item must carry, and the fees and postage costs that apply when it sells — plus a margin of profit on top of all of them. Most undercharging happens because one of those four is left out, usually the time, and the price then looks competitive while quietly losing money on every order. Work the price out from your own costs, in writing, before you look at anyone else's prices; then check it against comparable handmade work, adjust it for what makes your item different, and write the listing so the buyer can see why the item costs what it does. The method in this guide works for any handmade item, from a small sewn piece to a large piece of furniture, because it starts from the item in front of you rather than from a price someone else chose. Why pricing is the hardest part of selling handmade work

Pricing handmade work is hard for a reason that has nothing to do with arithmetic. The maker knows the item in a way no buyer does: they know how long it took, how many attempts failed before this one worked, how the materials felt in their hands, and how much of themselves went into the finish. That closeness makes the price feel personal. A low price can feel like an apology for the work, or like a way of asking the buyer to be kind. A high price can feel like a claim the maker is not sure they are allowed to make. And so many makers avoid the decision altogether: they copy the price of the cheapest similar item they can find, or they add a small amount to the cost of the materials and call that the price, or they ask a friend what they would pay and take that as the answer. Each of those shortcuts feels easier than working the price out, and each of them fails in the same way, because none of them starts from the maker's own costs. A price copied from another shop belongs to another shop's costs, materials, speed, and business. A price based only on materials ignores the hours that are, for most handmade work, the largest cost of all. A price based on a friend's guess is a guess about what one person might pay, not a calculation of what the business needs to survive. The result is familiar to anyone who has sold handmade work for a while: a shop that is busy, that gets good reviews, that the maker enjoys running, and that somehow never has any money left at the end of the month. The orders are real, the work is real, and the profit is missing, because the price was set without ever asking what the work actually costs. The fix is not a cleverer pricing trick. It is a habit: working the price out, on paper, from the item itself, every time, before the listing is written. That habit takes a few minutes per item once it is learned, and it changes the shop more than any other single habit, because a shop whose prices cover their costs can afford to keep making, to improve the work, and to turn down the orders that would lose money. A shop whose prices do not cover their costs cannot, however busy it looks.

The five parts of a price that earns a profit

Every handmade price is made of five parts, and a price that earns a profit is a price in which all five are present and none of them is guessed at. The first part is materials: the cost of the materials in that one item, measured as accurately as the maker can measure it, including the small things that are easy to forget — the thread, the glue, the fastenings, the lining, the finish, the label, and the share of any material bought in bulk that this item used. The second part is time: the hours the item takes, from the first cut or the first stitch to the finished, checked, packed item, charged at an hourly rate the maker has chosen deliberately, not at whatever rate the finished price happens to leave. The third part is overheads: the share of the costs that keep the shop running and that belong, in part, to every item — the tools and their replacement, the workspace, the photography, the listing time, the messages, the bookkeeping, and the consumables that are not part of any single item. The fourth part is the selling costs: the fees that apply when an item is listed and when it sells, and the postage and packaging costs where the seller pays them, described here without figures because fee levels change and must always be checked against the platform's current schedule before a price is set. The fifth part is profit: the margin left over after the first four parts are paid, which is what pays for growth, for the quiet months, for new tools, and for the maker's future, and which is not the same thing as the maker's wages. Wages pay for the hours worked. Profit pays for the business itself. A price that covers the first four parts but leaves no profit will keep the maker busy and the business still, because there will be nothing left to invest in the next step. A price that covers all five will feel higher than a copied price, and it should, because it is doing a job the copied price was never asked to do: it is paying for the work, the costs, and the future of the shop, all at once.

Step one: work out the true cost of materials

Materials are the easiest part of a price to get wrong, because the cost of the materials in one item is rarely the price written on the packet they came in. Start with the item in front of you, and list what went into it, in the quantities it actually used. If a skein of yarn cost a certain amount and the item used a third of it, the material cost is a third of the skein, not the whole skein. If a sheet of leather made three bags, each bag carries a third of the sheet. If a bottle of finish will coat twenty items, each item carries a twentieth of the bottle. That sounds fussy, and for the first item it is, but it is the only way to know what the item actually cost, and the habit becomes quick once the maker has worked it out for their usual materials. The second part of the material cost is the small materials that are easy to forget because they are cheap individually: the thread, the buttons, the clasps, the glue, the sandpaper, the varnish, the tissue paper, the care card, and the label. Individually they cost very little. Added together, across a whole item, they can cost more than the main material, and a price that forgets them will be short by that amount on every order. The third part is waste and mistakes. Materials are spoiled, cut wrong, or used in a test piece, and the cost of that waste belongs, in part, to the items that sell. A maker who throws away one piece in five should carry a share of that waste in the price of the four that sell, or the shop will slowly lose money on the material it never sold. The fourth part is the cost of getting the materials: the delivery charges, the trip to buy them, and the minimum quantities that leave the maker holding stock that may never be used. A material that is only sold in a large quantity, of which the maker uses a little, carries a real cost in tied-up money and storage, and the price should reflect the material as it is actually bought, not as it would be bought in a perfect world. Keep a simple materials record for each product: the materials, the quantities, and the cost per item, written down once and checked when a supplier's price changes. That record is what makes repricing possible later, because the maker who knows the material cost of an item can see at once whether a rise in a supplier's price has eaten the profit, and by how much. The maker who does not know it will only discover the problem months later, when the money has already gone.

Step two: charge for your time — honestly

Time is the part of the price that makers resist most, and it is the part that matters most. The resistance usually comes from one of three beliefs, and each of them deserves to be examined plainly. The first belief is that the time does not count because the maker enjoys the work. Enjoying work does not make it free. A maker who enjoys making and charges nothing for the hours will still need to eat, to pay for the workspace, and to replace the tools, and the hours that are given away are hours that cannot be spent on the work that would pay for those things. The second belief is that the time does not count because the maker is slow, or still learning. A learner's time is still time, and a learner who charges nothing for it will never earn enough to become faster, because they will be too busy working for nothing to practise the work that would make them faster. The third belief is that buyers will not pay for the time, so it is better to leave it out. Buyers do not pay for hours. They pay for the item, and the item's price is where the hours must be carried. A buyer who values handmade work understands, when the listing explains it, that the item took hours to make, and that the price reflects those hours. A buyer who does not value it will buy the mass-produced alternative, and no price the maker sets will keep them. The method is simple. Decide on an hourly rate for making time, deliberately, as the rate the work needs to earn to be sustainable. Time the making of the item, honestly, from the first step to the last, including the finishing, the checking, and the packing that belong to that item. Multiply the hours by the rate. That is the labour cost of the item, and it belongs in the price before anything else is added. If the total feels high, the honest responses are to make the work faster, to simplify the design, to choose materials that need less handwork, or to explain the value better in the listing — not to delete the time from the calculation. A price without the time in it is not a low price. It is a price that asks the maker to work for nothing, and a shop built on that price will not last, however much the maker loves the work.

Step three: overheads, fees and postage — the costs that hide

Overheads, fees and postage are the costs that hide because they do not belong to any single item, and a price that forgets them will be short by their total on every order. Overheads are the costs of keeping the shop running: the tools and their replacement, the workspace and its running costs, the photography and the listing time, the messages and the customer service, the bookkeeping and the record-keeping, and the consumables that serve every item rather than one. None of these can be assigned neatly to a single listing, so the practical method is to work out what they cost over a month, divide that by the number of items the shop expects to sell in that month, and carry that share in every price. A shop that sells few items must carry a larger share in each price than a shop that sells many, which is one reason a small shop's prices must be higher than a large shop's for the same item, and why comparing a small shop's price with a large shop's price is rarely a fair comparison. Fees are the platform's charges, and they must be allowed for in the price, without being stated here as figures, because fee schedules change and the only reliable source for the current levels is the platform's own published schedule, checked at the time the price is set. As a matter of structure, a seller should expect charges for listing an item, charges that apply when an item sells, and charges for payment processing, and should work out, for their own shop, what those charges amount to on a typical order, so the price can carry them. Advertising charges, where the seller chooses to use promoted listings, are a separate choice with a separate cost, and our guide to promoted listings covers whether that cost is worth paying; the important point for pricing is that advertising should be treated as a cost to be allowed for, not as a surprise to be discovered later. Postage and packaging must be handled just as deliberately. If the buyer pays postage separately, the listing must say so plainly, and the postage charged should reflect the real cost of sending that item, packed properly, to that destination. If postage is included in the price, the price must carry the full cost of the postage and the packaging, for the heaviest and furthest destination the listing offers, not for the cheapest. Packaging itself is a cost, not a free extra: the box, the wrapping, the protection, and the label all cost money, and all of it belongs in the price or in the postage charge, stated honestly. A seller who absorbs postage without allowing for it in the price is giving away part of the item's profit on every order, and will feel it most on the orders that travel furthest.

A simple pricing formula you can use for every item

With the costs worked out, the formula is straightforward, and it is worth writing it down and using it the same way for every item, so that prices across the shop are consistent and can be checked. The formula is: materials, plus labour at the chosen hourly rate, plus the share of overheads, plus the selling costs, multiplied or added to by a margin for profit. Some sellers add the profit as a percentage on top of the total cost, so that a more expensive item carries a larger absolute profit. Others add a fixed amount, so that every item contributes the same profit whatever its cost. Either method works, and the choice depends on the shop: a percentage margin suits a shop whose items vary widely in cost, because it keeps the profit proportional; a fixed margin suits a shop whose items are similar in cost and effort, because it is simpler to apply and to explain. What matters is not which method is chosen, but that a margin is chosen, written down, and applied consistently, so that the shop's prices are not set by feel on one day and by panic on another. Work an example through, with real numbers from your own shop, before you set a price. Take one item, list its materials at their true cost per item, time it honestly, multiply by your hourly rate, add the overhead share and the selling costs, and add the margin. The total is the price that item needs to earn a profit. Then, and only then, compare it with the prices of comparable handmade items, using the methods in our keyword research guide to find the phrases buyers use and the listings they see. If your price is higher than the comparable listings, ask why, and answer honestly: are your materials better, is the finish finer, is the item custom, does it take longer because it is done more carefully, or is your hourly rate higher than theirs because your costs are higher? If the difference is real, the listing must explain it, in words and photographs, so the buyer can see it. If the difference is not real — if the item is the same, made the same way, from the same materials — then the price, or the way the item is made, should change. The formula is not a machine that produces the right price by itself. It is a way of making the decision visible, so that the maker can see what the price must cover, and can choose, deliberately, whether to charge it, to change the item, or to accept a smaller margin on that one item for a stated reason. A deliberate choice, made with the numbers in front of you, is a pricing strategy. A price copied from another shop is not.

Reading the market without copying it

Checking comparable prices is a necessary part of pricing, and copying them is the mistake that ruins it. The check should be done the way a buyer does it: search for the item in the words a buyer would use, look at the listings that appear, and read them as a buyer would. Note the prices, but note much more than the prices. Note the materials, and whether they match yours. Note the size, and whether the comparable item is larger or smaller. Note the finish, the custom options, and the work that is visible in the photographs. Note whether the item is made to order, made in batches, or ready to send. Note the shop's reviews, and what buyers praise or complain about. And note whether the comparable item is genuinely handmade in the same way yours is, or whether it is assembled, printed, or finished by a process that takes far less handwork. Two items that look alike in a thumbnail can be very different items, and their prices should be different too. The vintage side of the shop teaches the same lesson: our vintage sellers guide shows how era, condition and provenance change what an item is worth, and the same is true of handmade work, where the materials, the method and the finish change what an item costs to make. Use the market check to place your price, not to set it. If your worked-out price sits within the range of comparable handmade items, the market is confirming your calculation, and the listing's job is to show why your item belongs at that price. If your price sits above the range, the market is asking a question: what is different about this item, and is the difference worth paying for? Answer that question in the listing, with photographs that show the difference and words that name it. If your price sits below the range, the market is asking a different question: is this item as good as the others, and why is it cheaper? A price that is too low can damage trust as surely as a price that is too high, because buyers associate a very low price with cheap materials, rushed work, or a shop that will not last. Set the price from your costs, use the market to check it, and explain the difference where there is one. That is reading the market. Copying the cheapest price you find is not reading the market. It is letting a stranger set your prices for you, without knowing your costs.

Discounts, sales and the price the buyer actually sees

Discounts and sales are part of selling on Etsy, and they must be planned for in the price, or they will eat the profit they were meant to create. The first rule is simple: a discount should come out of the profit margin, not out of the costs. A price that has no margin in it cannot be discounted without losing money, so a shop that discounts constantly from prices that were set too low is a shop that is paying buyers to take its work. The second rule is that a sale price should be a real price for a real reason, stated honestly. A seasonal sale, a clearance of a discontinued design, a thank-you for returning buyers, and a launch price for a new product are all real reasons, and buyers understand them. A permanent sale, where every item is always discounted from a price it was never sold at, is not a sale. It is a higher list price with a lower real price, and buyers learn to ignore it, while the maker learns to set prices that mean nothing. Our holiday listings guide covers the seasonal side of selling, and the pricing lesson from it is the same: plan the seasonal prices in advance, decide what will be discounted and by how much, and make sure the discounted price still covers the costs. The third rule is about custom work and personalisation. Custom work should cost more than the standard item, not the same, because it takes more time, more messages, more checking, and more risk of a remake. State the custom charge plainly, in the listing, before the buyer orders, and charge for the extra time at the same hourly rate as the rest of the work. A buyer who asks for a custom version and is told the price plainly will either accept it, which is good, or decline it, which is also good, because the work would have lost money at the standard price. The fourth rule is about free postage. Free postage is not free. It is postage paid by the seller, out of the price, and it should only be offered where the price carries it, for every destination the listing offers it to. A seller who offers free postage on a heavy item to a distant destination, without allowing for the postage in the price, will discover the cost on the first such order. Offer free postage deliberately, where it helps the buyer decide, and where the numbers allow it — or charge postage honestly, and let the buyer see the true cost of sending the item safely. Either approach is honest. Offering free postage and then sending the item badly packed, to save the cost, is not.

What to write in the listing so the price makes sense

A price is only part of what a buyer sees. The rest is the listing, and the listing is where the price is justified, or where it is left to look expensive without explanation. The listing should show, in words and in photographs, the work that the price pays for. Name the materials, and name them specifically: the kind of wood, the weight of the yarn, the type of leather, the finish that was used, and why that material was chosen. Name the process, in plain words: how the item is made, how many steps it takes, and what is done by hand rather than by machine. Name the time, where it helps: an item that takes several hours, or several days including drying and curing, is a different item from one that is assembled in minutes, and the buyer should know it. Show the work in the photographs: close-ups of the finish, of the joints, of the stitching, of the details that a cheap version would not have. A photograph of the maker's hands at work, where it fits the shop's style, can do more for a price than a paragraph of explanation, because it shows the buyer, in one image, that a person made this item, with skill, over time. The title and the tags should carry the words that describe the item's quality, not only its kind, following the method in our titles, tags and descriptions guide and our 13-tags guide: the material words, the method words, and the use words that a buyer who values handmade work will search for. Do not apologise for the price in the listing, and do not explain it defensively. A listing that says "I know this seems expensive, but" teaches the buyer to think the price is too high. A listing that says, calmly, what the item is made of, how it is made, and what it will do for the buyer, teaches the buyer to value it. If the item is offered in several sizes, several colours, or several levels of finish, price each variation from its own costs, and make the differences clear, so the buyer who chooses the larger or more detailed version understands why it costs more. And if the item is a gift, or is often bought as a gift, say so, and say how it will arrive: the packaging, the care card, and the absence of a price in the parcel are all part of what the buyer is paying for, and all of them belong in the listing.

When to raise prices, and how to do it without losing buyers

Prices should rise, over time, and a shop that never raises them is a shop whose profit is being quietly eaten by rising costs, improving skill, and growing demand. The triggers for a rise are practical. Costs rise: a supplier raises a price, a material becomes harder to get, or the postage for a common destination goes up. Skill rises: the work is better than it was a year ago, the finish is finer, the designs are stronger, and the item is worth more than it was. Demand rises: orders regularly exceed the time available, the waiting list grows, and the maker is turning work away that could have been taken at a higher price. Or the numbers say so: a review of the shop's figures shows that the current price, after every cost, leaves less profit than the shop needs, or no profit at all. When one of those triggers is present, raise the price, and do it deliberately. Raise it in small steps, and watch the effect, rather than doubling it overnight and guessing whether the change or something else caused the result. Raise the prices of the items that are affected, rather than raising everything at once, so the effect can be read clearly. And pair the rise, where possible, with a visible improvement: better materials, a finer finish, better photographs, a clearer listing, or a new variation that justifies the new price. A price rise that follows a visible improvement is understood by buyers, because they can see what the extra money buys. A price rise with no change at all will be questioned, and the questioning will come in the form of fewer orders, not complaints, so the maker may not know why the orders fell. For returning buyers, no announcement is usually needed for a small rise, but the listing itself should always explain the value, so that a buyer who returns after a gap sees the same item, better described, at a price that makes sense. If a buyer asks why a price has risen, answer honestly and briefly: the materials cost more, the work takes longer because it is done more carefully, or the item has been improved. Honesty about a price rise is respected. Silence about it, or a rise hidden in a changed listing, is not. And remember that raising prices is not the only response to rising costs. Simplifying a design, buying materials more efficiently, or making a process faster can all restore a margin without raising the price, and a maker who can do those things will need to raise prices less often. The choice between raising the price and changing the work is a real choice, and it should be made with the numbers in front of you, not by habit.

Variants, bundles and made-to-order: pricing the whole shop

Most handmade shops sell more than one version of an item, and the pricing must work across the whole shop, not only for a single listing. Variants — different sizes, colours, materials, or levels of detail — should each be priced from their own costs. A larger size uses more material and more time, and it should cost more, by the amount of that extra material and time, not by a round figure chosen to look tidy. A more expensive material should carry its extra cost, and the listing should say which material is which, so the buyer is choosing knowingly. Bundles — two or three items sold together at a combined price — should be priced from the combined costs, with a small saving for the buyer where the bundle genuinely saves the maker time or postage, and no saving where it does not. A bundle that is priced below the combined cost of its items, in the hope of selling more, is a bundle that loses money on every sale, and the more of them that sell, the worse the shop's position becomes. Made-to-order work deserves its own pricing rule, because it carries costs that ready-made work does not: the messages, the checking, the risk of a remake, and the time the item spends in the making queue while other work waits. Charge for the custom work at the same hourly rate, state the charge before the order is placed, and take a deposit or full payment in the way the platform allows, so that the maker is not left with a personalised item that cannot be sold to anyone else. The whole-shop check, run through our Etsy shop SEO audit checklist, is a good moment to check prices as well as listings: go through every listing, check that the price still covers the current material cost, the current time, and the current fees, and update the ones that no longer do. Shops drift. Materials change price, processes get faster or slower, and a listing priced a year ago may no longer be priced for the shop as it is today. A regular pricing review — once a season, or whenever a supplier's price changes — keeps the shop's prices honest, and keeps the maker from discovering, months later, that a popular item has been losing money all along. For a new shop, the same review should be done more often, because a new shop's costs and times are still being learned, and the first prices are estimates that the real figures will correct. Mistakes that cost handmade sellers money

  • Leaving time out of the price — the largest cost in most handmade work, given away on every order.
  • Copying the cheapest similar price — a price that belongs to another shop's costs, materials and speed, not yours.
  • Forgetting the small materials — thread, fastenings, finish, packaging and labels, which add up across a whole item.
  • Forgetting the fees — listing, selling and payment charges that must be allowed for, checked against the current schedule.
  • Offering free postage without allowing for it — the postage comes out of the profit, most painfully on the furthest orders.
  • Discounting from a price with no margin — a sale that loses money, however many orders it brings in.
  • Charging the same for custom work as for standard work — the extra messages, checking and remake risk are real work, and real cost.
  • Never raising prices — rising costs and improving skill quietly eat the profit until there is none left.
  • Apologising for the price in the listing — a defensive listing teaches the buyer to think the price is too high.
  • Pricing a bundle below the cost of its items — the more bundles that sell, the more the shop loses.

Pricing handmade work for profit is not a talent, and it is not a trick. It is a habit: working out, for each item, what it costs in materials, in time, in overheads and in selling costs, adding a margin for profit, checking the result against comparable handmade work, and writing the listing so the buyer can see why the item costs what it does. Do that for one item, with real numbers, and the next item will be easier. Do it for the whole shop, and the shop will change: the prices will be higher than the copied prices were, the orders will be fewer in number and better in value, and the money left at the end of the month will be real, because it will have been planned for rather than hoped for. Start with the item you sell most, or the item you make most often. Time it, cost it, price it by the formula, and rewrite its listing to show the work the price pays for, using the title and tag methods in our titles, tags and descriptions guide and the keyword methods in our keyword research guide. Then work through the rest of the shop, one item at a time, keeping the cost record for each one as you go. The record is the shop's real pricing asset: it is what makes the next price faster, the next rise easier to justify, and the next sale a sale that earns money rather than giving it away. A handmade business that prices its work honestly can afford to keep making, to improve, and to grow. A handmade business that does not will, sooner or later, have to choose between raising its prices and stopping. Choose the honest price now, while the choice is still yours to make.

Frequently asked questions

How should I price a handmade item on Etsy?
Start with the real cost of making it: the materials used, the time spent at a fair hourly rate, and the share of the costs that every item must carry, such as packaging, tools, and the fees that apply when the item sells. Add a margin for profit on top of that total, so the price leaves something after every cost is paid. Then check the price against comparable handmade items, and adjust it for what makes yours different — the materials, the finish, the custom work, or the time it takes — rather than copying a competitor's price. A price worked out from your own costs will survive a busy month; a price copied from someone else's shop may not cover your costs at all.

Why do so many handmade sellers undercharge on Etsy?
Most undercharging comes from leaving something out of the calculation. Sellers count the materials but not the hours, or count the hours at a rate they would never accept from an employer, or forget the fees, the packaging, and the time spent on messages, photographs, and listing the item. Others set a price by looking at the cheapest similar item, and then try to match it, without knowing whether that seller's costs, materials, or business model are anything like their own. The result is a price that feels competitive and loses money quietly, one order at a time. Working the price out from your own costs, in writing, is the habit that stops it.

Should I charge for my time when pricing handmade work?
Yes. Time is the largest cost in most handmade work, and leaving it out means working for nothing once the materials are paid for. Decide on an hourly rate for your making time — the rate you would need to earn to make the work sustainable — and multiply it by the hours the item takes, including the finishing, the checking, and the packing that belong to that item. If the resulting price feels high, the answer is usually to make the work faster, to simplify the design, or to explain the value better in the listing, not to remove the time from the calculation. A price that pays for your time is what turns a hobby that sells into a business that lasts.

When should I raise my Etsy prices?
Raise prices when your costs rise, when your skill or materials improve, when demand regularly exceeds the time you have, or when a review of your numbers shows the current price no longer leaves a profit after every cost. Raise them in small, deliberate steps, and update the listings that are affected, rather than changing everything at once without checking the effect. Tell returning buyers nothing they do not need to know, but do explain the value in the listing itself: better materials, more detail, or work that takes longer because it is done properly. A price rise that follows a visible improvement in the work is understood by buyers; a price rise that follows no change at all will be questioned.
